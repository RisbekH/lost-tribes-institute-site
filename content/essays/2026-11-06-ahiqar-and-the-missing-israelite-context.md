An important figure who appears in Assyrian, Persian or Babylonian records is labeled Assyrian, Persian or Babylonian, and the conversation ends there. But those empires were not sealed rooms. They were full of deported and resettled peoples, and from the 8th century BC on, one of those resettled populations was Israel. Across Asia, historical figures and events are regularly discussed with no mention of the Israelites who lived alongside them, served in the same courts and spoke the same languages. Ahiqar, the wise counselor of the Assyrian kings, is one clear example.

## The usual label

Ahiqar is normally described as part of an Assyrian court tradition, and history places him at the courts of Sennacherib and Esarhaddon in the 7th century BC. Both kings also appear in the Bible: Sennacherib in 2 Kings 18–19 and Isaiah 36–37, and Esarhaddon in 2 Kings 19:37, Isaiah 37:38 and Ezra 4:2. But the oldest copy we have is not in Akkadian, the language of Assyrian scholarship. It is in Aramaic, on a 5th-century BC papyrus from Elephantine in Egypt, and the people who kept it were a Judean military colony.

James Lindenberger's standard study of the proverbs (*The Aramaic Proverbs of Ahiqar*, Johns Hopkins, 1983) argued from their dialect and the gods they name (El, Shamayn, Shamash) that the sayings come from Syria, the West Semitic world encompassing Israel and others, not from native Mesopotamia. Ahiqar is not a pure Assyrian product. He is a West Semitic figure working inside the Assyrian system, and that system is exactly where the Israelite deportees ended up.

## The language question

A common objection is that the northern Israelites spoke Hebrew, not Aramaic, so they could have had no part in an Aramaic tradition. That argument backfires.

- **Northern speech was not Jerusalem speech.** The Samaria ostraca and other northern Israelite texts show a dialect of its own, and linguists have long noted that northern Hebrew shares features with Aramaic. The Shibboleth story in Judges 12 shows that the tribes themselves heard the difference.
- **The north lived beside Aram.** Northern Israel bordered Aram-Damascus for generations, through war, trade and marriage. The Tel Dan stele, found in northern Israel, is written in Aramaic, and the Deir Alla text from Gilead is written in a local language that sits between Aramaic and Hebrew. In Galilee and Gilead, daily life was very likely bilingual.
- **Aramaic was the empire's common language.** By the late 8th century it was the shared language of the Assyrian Empire. In 2 Kings 18:26 (701 BC), Judean officials ask the Assyrian envoy to speak Aramaic, which shows the elite already used it.
- **Exile settled the matter.** Deportees resettled in Gozan, Halah and the Habor region lived in Aramaic every day. Within a generation they were part of the same Aramaic-speaking population that carried the Ahiqar tradition.

## Israelites inside Assyria

The Israelite presence in Assyria is not a guess. It is in the Assyrian records themselves.

- In his Great Summary Inscription, Sargon II says he took 27,290 people from Samaria and gathered 50 chariots from them. Samarian chariot crews were put into the Assyrian army.
- Assyrian documents from the late 8th and 7th centuries contain names known from the Bible as Israelite, especially names built on the divine name Yau (YHWH).
- A commercial contract from Gozan, dated to the end of the 8th century, is witnessed by two high-ranking Assyrian officials, one named Nadbiyau (biblical Nedabiah). Another document names the officials Palṭiyau and Niriyau (biblical Pelatiah and Neriah), who almost certainly were Israelites.
- These records suggest that the exiles were generally not slaves or an oppressed underclass. K. Lawson Younger's survey of the evidence carries the subtitle "Their Names Appear at All Levels of Assyrian Society."
- Jewish tradition later claimed Ahiqar outright. The book of Tobit makes him the nephew of Tobit, an exile from the tribe of Naphtali living in Nineveh.

## Older wisdom, newer neighbors

Mesopotamian wisdom literature is far older than the fall of Samaria, and no one needs to claim that Israelites invented it. The question is narrower: what world produced Ahiqar in the 8th and 7th centuries BC? His proverbs are closer in form and content to biblical Proverbs than to the old Sumerian collections, which is why scholars compare the two so often.

## What the evidence supports

By Ahiqar's time, Israelites had been part of Assyrian society for two generations, including in the army and the civil service. They lived in Aramaic, the language in which Ahiqar has come down to us, and they belonged to the West Semitic world his proverbs come from. Cultural exchange in that setting is not speculation. It is the normal outcome of what the records show. What cannot be proven is a specific Israelite hand in the Ahiqar text, or whether Ahiqar himself was an Israelite, and that is not the claim.

The claim is simpler. Ahiqar cannot be fully understood without the Israelites who lived around him, and he is far from the only figure in Asian history of whom that is true.
