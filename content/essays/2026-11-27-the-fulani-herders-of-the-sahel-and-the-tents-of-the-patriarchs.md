Before the sun clears the thorn trees, the camp is already awake. Calves strain against a long rope staked out in the sand. A woman walks down the line with a calabash, lets each calf suck for a moment to start the milk, then pulls it away and milks the cow herself. Smoke from the night fire drifts over the herd. A young man stands at the edge of the camp leaning on his staff, saying almost nothing. Then the cattle move out, and he moves with them, toward grass he has never seen but his father told him about.

This is a morning among the Fulani, somewhere in the Sahel between Senegal and Lake Chad. Swap the calabash for a clay jar and the thorn trees for the oaks of Mamre, and it could be a morning in the camp of Abraham.

## Who they are

The Fulani, who call themselves Fulɓe and whom the French call Peul, are some 40 million people spread across West and Central Africa. Most today are farmers, traders and city people. A smaller part, such as the Woɗaaɓe of Niger and Nigeria, still live with their herds and move with the rains. Their language, Fulfulde or Pulaar, belongs to the Atlantic branch of the Niger-Congo family, close to Wolof and Serer. Most historians place their homeland in Senegambia, in the valley called Futa Toro, from where they spread east over the last thousand years.

They have made history as well as kept herds. Fulani scholars led the reform movements that built Muslim states in Futa Jallon, Masina and, in 1804, the Sokoto Caliphate in what is now northern Nigeria, one of the largest states in Africa in its time.

## Their own origin story

Ask about beginnings and you will often hear a story set down in the Islamic era. An Arab commander named Uqba came west with the armies of Islam. He married a local woman, Bajjo Mangu. Their sons became the four founding clans of the Fulani: Jallo, Bâ, Sow and Bari. Many Fulani accept this story today.

In some tellings the ancestress came from Palestine. And in the history of the Fulani written by Waziri Alhaji Junaidu of Sokoto in 1957, the ruling Toronkawa line runs back to Abraham, Isaac and Esau.

European writers added their own layer. In 1912 the French colonial scholar Maurice Delafosse proposed that the Fulani descended from people of Jewish origin who fled Roman persecution through Cyrenaica and the Fezzan, crossed the Sahara and became advisers, then masters, of the Soninke kings of ancient Ghana. Others guessed Egypt, Ethiopia, the Berbers, even India.

So there are three stories here, and it matters who tells which: a Fulani story of an Arab father and a local mother; a Fulani genealogy that reaches Abraham through Esau; and a European theory of Jewish wanderers.

## The Institute's reading: a herder's Bible

What follows is our reading. It is not proof. It is a set of mirrors, held up between the Fulani camp and the tents of Genesis.

**Cattle as a family, not a commodity.** The anthropologist Derrick Stenning, who lived with the Woɗaaɓe in the 1950s, wrote that their cattle are sold only to meet an overriding need for cash, and meat is eaten only on ceremonial and ritual occasions. Milk, not meat, is daily food. That is the economy of the patriarchs. Abraham was rich in livestock (Genesis 13:2). When Jacob's family stood before Pharaoh, they said, in effect, "We are herders, and so were our fathers" (Genesis 46:32–34; 47:3). In Israel, too, meat was mostly eaten at a sacrifice or a feast; the law had to make special room for ordinary slaughter when people lived far from the sanctuary (Deuteronomy 12:20–21).

**When the herds grow, the family splits.** Abraham and his nephew Lot had so many animals that the land could not hold them both. So Abraham said: there is no need to fight, the whole land is in front of you, you go one way and I will go the other (Genesis 13:6–9). Herding peoples everywhere know this moment. A lineage grows, the grass runs thin, and brothers part on good terms with their own share of the herd. The Fulani spread across 3,000 miles of Sahel this way, one move at a time.

**Keeping the herd inside the lineage.** Stenning describes Fulani lineages that keep a partial endogamy through preferred cousin marriage, and widow inheritance that keeps cattle in the father's line. Genesis is full of the same logic. Abraham sends his servant back to his own kin to find Isaac a wife (Genesis 24:4). Jacob marries his cousins Leah and Rachel (Genesis 29). The daughters of Zelophehad may marry whom they like, but within their father's clan, so that the inheritance does not pass to another tribe (Numbers 36:6–9). And when a man dies childless, his brother marries the widow to keep his name and his land alive (Deuteronomy 25:5–10), the custom at the heart of the book of Ruth.

**Four clans, four directions.** Fulani tradition ties the four founding clans to colors and to the points of the compass. Israel camped the same way, in four divisions around the tent of meeting, each under its own banner and facing its own side: Judah to the east, Reuben to the south, Ephraim to the west, Dan to the north (Numbers 2). A nation drawn as a square around a sacred center is not common. It is worth noticing where it shows up.

**Patience as the mark of a true person.** The Fulani code is called pulaaku, "Fulani-ness." Writers usually sum it up in three words: munyal, patience; semteende, a sense of shame or modesty; and hakkille, good sense. A true Pullo does not shout, does not show hunger, does not lose his temper in public. Proverbs could be its handbook: better to be patient than to be a warrior, and better to rule your temper than to capture a city (Proverbs 16:32). The fool shows his anger at once; the wise one covers an insult (Proverbs 12:16).

**Solomon in the cattle camp.** In Koumen, the pastoral initiation text that Amadou Hampâté Bâ and Germaine Dieterlen published in 1961, the young man who learns the secrets of the herds and becomes the first priest of the cattle is called Silé, a form of the name Solomon, and the secrets are guarded by a serpent. Solomon in the Hebrew Bible is remembered for exactly this kind of knowledge: he spoke of trees and of beasts, birds, creeping things and fish (1 Kings 4:33). Islam knows Solomon too, so the name may have arrived with Muslim teachers. But it is striking that the Fulani chose to set him at the gate of their oldest herding lore.

**An Esau line, not a Jacob line.** Junaidu's genealogy deserves careful reading. It runs through Esau, not Jacob. In the Hebrew Bible, Esau is Jacob's twin, the hunter and herdsman who settled in Seir and became Edom (Genesis 36). If the Fulani memory is taken on its own terms, it does not claim to be a lost tribe at all. It claims to be family, Israel's brother. That is a more modest claim, and in some ways a more interesting one.

## Mirrors on the map

The Fulani are not alone. On the Institute's map, the Kalenjin of Kenya remember coming "out of Misri." The Nuer of South Sudan live by cattle and pray over them in ways that have reminded readers of Leviticus. The Yibir of Somalia carry a name that echoes "Hebrew." And far to the east, the Kyrgyz herders of the Tian Shan keep the same rhythm of summer pasture and winter camp, honor the same patience, and tell their own stories of patriarchs.

Herders talk to herders across continents. The question is whether the likeness comes from a shared way of life, or from a shared memory as well.

## Where a skeptic will push back

A fair reader should hear the other side, and it is strong.

**Language.** In the early twentieth century many Europeans called the Fulani "Hamitic" and linked their language to the Afro-Asiatic family, which includes Hebrew, Arabic and Berber. In the mid-twentieth century the linguist Joseph Greenberg showed that Fulfulde belongs to the Niger-Congo family instead. There is no Hebrew layer in the language.

**The legends are late and Islamic.** The Uqba story names an Arab Muslim general of the seventh century. It reads like a way of joining the Fulani to the family of Islam, not a memory of ancient Israel. The historian Andrew Clark notes that some Fulani also took early European theories of a Middle Eastern origin into their own traditions, so it can be hard to know which parts are old.

**Colonial baggage.** The "Hamitic" theory ranked Africans by how "foreign" their ancestors seemed, and placed the Fulani above their neighbors. Delafosse's Jewish theory grew in that same soil. Any lost-tribes reading must leave the ranking behind.

**The genetics point north, but earlier.** A 2025 study led by Cesar Fortes-Lima, with about 460 Fulani from 18 places, found a West African base plus an ancestry shared by all Fulani that is linked to ancient North Africans and to today's Berber-speaking Moroccans. A 2019 study found that the Fulani share their gene for digesting milk in adulthood with North Africans, likely through contact between herders. The researchers tie this history to the Green Sahara, when the desert was grassland, roughly 12,000 to 5,000 years ago. That is thousands of years before Abraham. The data show contact across the Sahara, not a migration from Israel.

**Herding looks like herding.** Cousin marriage, widow inheritance, milk diets and meat at feasts are found among pastoral peoples across Africa and Asia. They may show that Genesis describes a real herding world, not that the Fulani came from it.

## A closing word

None of this needs to be settled to respect what the Fulani have kept. They carried a code of patience and self-control across 3,000 miles of hard country. They kept their herds as family and their families as herds. And some of them remember a mother from Palestine and a father's line that runs back to Isaac's other son.

The Institute reads those memories as an open door, not a closed case. When a Fulani elder speaks of Esau, he is naming the brother Jacob wronged, the one who ran to meet him on the road home and threw his arms around him (Genesis 33:4). There may be no better picture for scattered families finding each other again.
