## Introduction

Rabbinic Judaism and Islam are probably the two closest religious systems in structure. Both center on living by a revealed law, interpreted by trained scholars, rather than on creeds and sacraments.

This paper sets out the main parallels: monotheism, law, revelation, legal method, practice and the afterlife. It then looks closely at four boundary markers both faiths share: the end of prophecy, a holy language, the rejection of Jesus as God incarnate, and the rejection of the New Testament as scripture. It closes with their historical influence on each other and the differences that still divide them.

## Strict monotheism

Both insist that God is absolutely one and cannot be divided. Judaism states it in the Shema: "Hear, O Israel, the Lord is our God, the Lord is One" (Deut. 6:4). Islam calls it *tawhid*, the oneness of God.

- Both reject any partner beside God, any division within him, and any incarnation.
- Both largely forbid images of God.
- Medieval scholars in both traditions debated how literally to read passages that describe God with human features, such as a hand, a face or a throne.

## Religion as law

Both faiths are built around a body of divine law that covers all of life. *Halakha* means "the way one walks." *Sharia* means "the path to water."

- Each covers prayer, food, marriage, business, inheritance, hygiene and civil disputes.
- Neither draws a sharp line between religious duty and everyday law.
- Faithfulness is measured mainly by practice, not by agreeing to a creed.

## Two layers of revelation

Each faith pairs a written scripture with a second body of teaching, without which the first cannot be put into practice.

|  | Rabbinic Judaism | Islam |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Written revelation | Written Torah | Qur'an |
| Second layer | Oral Torah, recorded in the Mishnah and Talmud | Sunna, the Prophet's example, recorded in hadith |
| Chain of authority | Mishnah Avot 1:1 traces it from Moses to the Men of the Great Assembly | Each hadith carries an *isnad*, its chain of narrators |
| Reluctance to write it down | Early sages resisted writing the oral law (Gittin 60b) | Early leaders, including Umar, hesitated to record hadith |

The need for the second layer is easy to see. The Torah forbids work on the Sabbath but never fully defines "work." The Qur'an commands prayer but never spells out how to perform it.

## Legal methods and institutions

The two legal systems developed nearly matching tools and institutions.

| Feature | Rabbinic Judaism | Islam |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authorities | Rabbis | *Ulama* |
| Reasoning by analogy | *Heqesh*, *kal va-chomer* | *Qiyas* |
| Consensus | Majority of the sages | *Ijma* |
| Local custom | *Minhag* | *'Urf* |
| Schools of law | Houses of Hillel and Shammai; later Ashkenazi and Sephardi practice | Sunni *madhhabs*: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali |
| Answer to a specific question | *Teshuvah* (responsum) | *Fatwa* |

In neither faith does authority depend on a sacramental priesthood. It rests on learning: a scholar's standing comes from mastery of the texts and the methods.

## Shared practices

Many daily practices line up closely.

- **Food:** kosher and halal rules both require slaughter by a cut to the throat, forbid pork and forbid consuming blood.
- **Circumcision** of males.
- **Prayer** at fixed times of day, facing a sacred direction: Jerusalem or Mecca.
- **Washing** before prayer, and rules of ritual purity.
- **Modesty** rules, and separation of men and women in worship.
- **Charity** as a duty, not just a virtue: *tzedakah* and *zakat*.

## Human nature and the afterlife

Both reject the idea that humans inherit sin at birth. A person is born innocent, with competing good and bad impulses: the rabbinic *yetzer ha-tov* and *yetzer ha-ra*, and the Qur'anic *nafs*, the self that can be trained or can lead astray.

- **Repentance** (*teshuvah* / *tawba*) restores a person directly to God, with no go-between required.
- **Free will and divine decree:** both traditions held long internal debates on how human choice fits with God's control.
- **The end:** both teach angels, bodily resurrection, a final judgment, reward and punishment, and a coming end-time redeemer. In Judaism that is the Messiah. In Islam it is the Mahdi, alongside the return of Jesus.

## Prophecy has ended

Both religions hold that God no longer speaks through prophets, and that authority passed to scholars who interpret a closed body of revelation.

### Rabbinic Judaism

- The Tosefta (Sotah 13:3) and the Babylonian Talmud (Yoma 9b, Sanhedrin 11a) teach that when Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi died, *ruach ha-kodesh*, the spirit of prophecy, left Israel. Only the *bat kol*, a faint heavenly echo, remained.
- Seder Olam Rabbah 30 places the end in the time of Alexander the Great. From then on, people were to listen to the words of the sages.
- Bava Batra 12a goes further: since the Temple's destruction, prophecy was taken from the prophets and given to the wise, and a sage is greater than a prophet.

### Islam

- The Qur'an calls Muhammad *khatam an-nabiyyin*, the "seal of the prophets" (33:40).
- A well-known hadith adds: "There is no prophet after me."
- Another hadith says the scholars are the heirs of the prophets, which mirrors the transfer described in Bava Batra.

### What follows in both systems

1. **Law can't be changed by new revelation.** In the "oven of Akhnai" story (Bava Metzia 59b), a heavenly voice sides with Rabbi Eliezer, and the sages overrule it: "It is not in heaven" (Deut. 30:12). Islamic law likewise holds that dreams, visions and mystical insight cannot establish a ruling.
2. **A newcomer who changes the law is a false prophet.** Maimonides (Yesodei HaTorah, ch. 9) rules that a claimed prophet who adds or removes a commandment is false. Islam treats later claims the same way, from Musaylima in Muhammad's own day to the Ahmadiyya and Baha'i movements.
3. **The end-time figure brings no new law.** Maimonides insists the Messiah will not alter the Torah. In Islamic belief, Jesus returns and rules by Muhammad's law rather than his own.

## A holy language

Each faith treats the language of its scripture as sacred: Hebrew, *lashon ha-kodesh* ("the holy tongue"), and Arabic, the language of the Qur'an.

### The language of revelation, and of creation

- Genesis Rabbah 18:4 argues the world was created in Hebrew, citing the wordplay *ish/ishah* (man/woman), which works only in Hebrew.
- Jewish mysticism, from Sefer Yetzirah onward, treats the Hebrew letters as the building blocks of creation.
- The Qur'an calls itself "an Arabic Qur'an" (12:2) in "clear Arabic speech" (26:195).
- Islamic doctrine holds the Qur'an's language cannot be imitated (*i'jaz*). A translation is not the Qur'an, only an interpretation of its meaning.

### Language in worship

- Arabic is required for the daily prayers. Abu Hanifa reportedly allowed Persian at first and later withdrew that ruling.
- Judaism is slightly more flexible in principle: Mishnah Sotah 7:1 allows the Shema, the central prayer and grace after meals in any language.
- In practice, Hebrew became the language of synagogue worship everywhere, whether Jews spoke Yiddish, Ladino or Arabic. A Torah scroll used in worship must be written in Hebrew in the square script.
- In both traditions, children who don't speak the holy language learn to read and recite it. The Muslim *hafiz* who memorizes the whole Qur'an has a parallel in Jewish children reading Torah portions in Hebrew.

### Respect for the written text

- Worn Hebrew texts containing God's name are stored in a *genizah* and buried. Worn Qur'ans are buried or respectfully burned.
- Jews avoid pronouncing the four-letter name of God and do not erase certain divine names. Muslims handle the written name *Allah* and copies of the Qur'an with matching care.

### Shared roots, literally

Both are Semitic languages built on three-consonant roots, so much of the core religious vocabulary matches.

| Meaning | Hebrew / Aramaic | Arabic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peace | *shalom* | *salam* |
| Charity | *tzedakah* | *sadaqa* |
| God | *Elohim*, *Eloah* | *Allah* |
| Prayer | *tzelota* (Aramaic) | *salat* |

The scientific study of Hebrew grammar grew directly out of Arabic grammar. In the 10th and 11th centuries, Judah ibn Hayyuj and Jonah ibn Janah, working in Islamic Spain, applied Arabic grammatical theory to establish the three-letter root system in Hebrew.

## Rejecting Jesus as God incarnate

Both faiths reject the Christian claims that Jesus is God in human form and that God is a Trinity. Each states this in terms aimed directly at those claims.

- **The Qur'an:** "He does not beget, nor was He begotten" (112:3). "Do not say 'Three'" (4:171). Those who call God "the third of three" have disbelieved (5:73).
- **The Jerusalem Talmud** (Taanit 2:1): Rabbi Abbahu says that a man who claims "I am God" is lying, and one who claims to be "the son of man" will regret it.
- **Exodus Rabbah 29:5** reads Isaiah 44:6 as God saying: I have no father, I have no son, I have no brother.
- **"Two powers in heaven"** (*shtei reshuyot*): the rabbis condemned the idea of any second divine figure beside God.

Medieval Jews and Muslims made this case together. Saadia Gaon's refutation of the Trinity (*Beliefs and Opinions*, book 2) uses the same philosophical arguments as Muslim theologians, and polemicists in each community borrowed from the other.

### Where they part ways on Jesus

|  | Islam | Rabbinic Judaism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Status | A major prophet (*Isa*), born of a virgin, given the title *al-Masih* (Messiah) | Not a prophet and not the Messiah |
| Death | Not crucified; it was only made to appear so (4:157) | Executed; Talmudic passages about "Yeshu" (e.g. Sanhedrin 43a) are disputed as to whom they mean |
| End times | Returns before the Judgment | No role |

Maimonides drew a telling line between the two. He classed Christianity as idolatry because of the Trinity and incarnation, but called Islam pure monotheism. In an uncensored passage of his law code (Melachim 11:4), he also suggested both religions served God's plan by spreading knowledge of the Torah and its commandments worldwide.

## The New Testament has no authority

Neither faith treats the New Testament as scripture, and it plays no part in either one's law or worship today.

### Judaism

- The Hebrew Bible's canon closed with no place for later books. Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1 warns against reading "outside books."
- The Talmud (Shabbat 116a) rules that the *gilyonim*, generally understood as early Christian scriptures, need not be saved from a fire on the Sabbath. It also mocks their Greek name with a Hebrew pun meaning "scroll of wickedness."
- Today, Jewish scholars study the New Testament as a historical source on first-century Judaism, but it carries no religious authority.

### Islam

- The Qur'an says God gave Jesus a revealed book, the *Injil*.
- Islamic teaching holds that the existing New Testament is not that book, or is a distorted form of it. This doctrine is called *tahrif* (alteration), based on verses such as 5:13.
- Early scholars mostly saw the distortion as misinterpretation. Later thinkers such as Ibn Hazm (11th century) argued the text itself was corrupted.
- Muslim writers mainly cite the New Testament in debates with Christians, for example reading the "Comforter" of John 14:16 as a prediction of Muhammad, connected to Qur'an 61:6.

### An important asymmetry

Islam applies the same doctrine of alteration to the Jewish Torah (*Tawrat*) and Psalms (*Zabur*). So Islam rejects not only the New Testament but the received Hebrew Bible as well.

Maimonides noticed this and drew a practical conclusion in one of his responsa. A Jew may teach Torah to Christians, who accept the Hebrew text and can be persuaded from it, but not to Muslims, who reject the text itself.

## Historical cross-influence

The parallels are not only structural. The two traditions shaped each other directly over many centuries.

- **Shared stories:** the Qur'an and the *Isra'iliyyat* tradition contain stories also found in Midrash, such as Abraham smashing his father's idols and Cain learning burial from a raven. Abraham Geiger made this case in 1833.
- **Theology in Arabic:** under Islamic rule, Saadia Gaon (10th century) wrote Jewish theology in Arabic using the methods of Islamic *kalam*.
- **Philosophy:** Maimonides built on the Muslim philosophers al-Farabi and Avicenna. His Thirteen Principles of faith echo Islamic creedal statements.
- **Grammar:** as noted above, Hebrew grammar as a science was modeled on Arabic grammar.

## Key differences

The parallels sit alongside real differences.

| Question | Rabbinic Judaism | Islam |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When did prophecy end? | With Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi, long before Muhammad | With Muhammad, its final "seal" |
| Who is the faith for? | One covenant people; other nations are expected to keep only the seven Noahide laws | All humanity, under the same faith and law |
| Status of the Hebrew Bible | The received text is authoritative | The received text is considered altered |
| Status of Jesus | Neither prophet nor Messiah | Prophet and Messiah, but not divine |
| Handling disagreement | The Talmud deliberately preserves losing opinions and open debates | Each school's law records mainly its conclusions |

## Conclusion

The parallels fit together into one picture. Both religions present themselves as a return to the original monotheism of Abraham and Moses. Both define themselves partly against Christianity's claims: a divine Son, a new covenant and a new scripture.

Both also close the door to revelation at a fixed point, leaving scholars to interpret a sacred text in a sacred language. Where they part ways is over Muhammad himself. For Judaism, the door had already closed before him; for Islam, he is the one who closed it.
