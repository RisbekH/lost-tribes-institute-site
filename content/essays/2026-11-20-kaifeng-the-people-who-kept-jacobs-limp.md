A butcher's shop in old Kaifeng. A leg of mutton lies on the block. The knife does not go for the joint. It goes deep along the back of the thigh and draws out a long pale cord, the sciatic nerve. The cord goes in the scrap bucket. The meat goes home to a family that hangs Chinese couplets on its door and sends its sons to sit the Confucian exams.

The neighbours watched this for centuries. They had a name for these people: *tiao jin jiao*, "the religion that plucks out the sinew."[^sinew] Of all the things the community did, that one cut is what China remembered.

The people themselves used another name. They called their faith *Yicileye*. Israel.[^name]

## Who they are

Kaifeng, on the Yellow River in Henan, was the capital of the Northern Song dynasty, one of the richest cities on earth. By about 1120, and probably earlier, a community of Jews had settled there.[^date] Most scholars think they came as merchants from Persia or Central Asia along the Silk Road. Judeo-Persian manuscripts are among the clues to where they came from.[^dna]

They built a synagogue. They kept the Sabbath, Passover and the Day of Atonement, and in their best years they read Hebrew from thirteen Torah scrolls.[^customs] Chinese neighbours called them the "blue-cap Hui," to tell them apart from the white-capped Muslims.[^bluecap]

Europe found out about them by accident. In June 1605 a scholar named Ai Tian, who had passed the provincial exams, called on the Jesuit Matteo Ricci in Beijing. He told Ricci of an Israelite congregation back home, a thousand strong.[^ricci]

Then came the long fade. The Yellow River flooded the city again and again. In 1642, during a siege, the dykes were broken and the city drowned; the synagogue, its books and its graveyard went under.[^flood] The families rebuilt and recopied their scrolls. But Hebrew learning thinned out. The last rabbi died around 1800. By the 1860s the synagogue had been taken down and its stones sold, and Western collectors bought up the scrolls.[^britannica]

Today perhaps 500 to 1,000 people in Kaifeng know they come from these families. In 1953 the state classed them as Han Chinese. A small revival of study in the 1990s and 2000s ended after 2015, when foreign Jewish groups were told to leave.[^forward] A few have moved to Israel.

## Their own story

The community told its own history in stone. The oldest surviving stele, set up in 1489, says their fathers came from Tianzhu, an old Chinese name for India, bringing Western cloth as tribute. The emperor received them and said: "You have come to Our China. Reverence and preserve the customs of your ancestors, and hand them down at Bianliang."[^stele] Bianliang is Kaifeng.

The stelae go further back than Kaifeng. They trace the faith to Abraham and Moses, and set those men in the early Zhou dynasty, on a Chinese calendar. A later stele of 1512 says the faith entered China in the Han era.[^zhou]

That is how they told it. A people from far away, told by a king to keep their fathers' customs, and writing it on stone.

## The Institute's reading

The connections below are the Institute's own reading. They are not proven fact.

**A people named after a wound.** In Genesis 32 Jacob wrestles all night with a man at the ford of the Jabbok. At dawn the man touches the socket of Jacob's hip, and Jacob limps away. He gets a new name, Israel, "one who struggles with God." And the text adds: that is why the children of Israel do not eat the sinew of the hip to this day (Genesis 32:24–32).

In Kaifeng both halves of that story survived. The people kept the name, *Yicileye*. Their neighbours named them after the limp. The Bible scholar Aaron Demsky notes how strange this is: a minor food rule became the people's whole identity in Chinese eyes.[^sinew] We would put it more simply. A wound remembered longer than a law code. Peoples on the map often keep the odd, physical custom long after the theology is gone. The Bene Israel of India kept the Sabbath and the *Shema* but had forgotten most of the rest. The custom is the last thing to go.

**The Hall of the Ancestors.** The Jesuit Jean-Paul Gozani, who lived in Kaifeng from 1698 to 1718, described a hall beside the synagogue. Incense bowls stood there for the patriarchs and the "holy men" of the community, Chinese style, with one difference: no images were allowed.[^gozani]

Hold that beside Exodus 3:15. God tells Moses to say he is "the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob," and adds: this is my name forever, the way I am to be remembered by every generation. Israel's God introduced himself through the family line. In China, where every household honours its ancestors, the Kaifeng families found a ready-made form for that. They did not break the second commandment to do it (Exodus 20:4). They kept the bowls and dropped the pictures.

The Kyrgyz on our map do something close: a man is expected to know his seven fathers by name. Different setting, same instinct. You know who you are by who came before you.

**Keep your fathers' customs.** The emperor's words on the 1489 stele sound a lot like another royal word to exiles. Jeremiah wrote to the captives in Babylon: build houses, plant gardens, marry, and seek the peace of the city where you have been sent (Jeremiah 29:4–7). Settle down, but stay yourselves. Cyrus, too, sent the Judeans home to keep their own law (Ezra 1:1–4). In the Bible, foreign kings often give Israel permission to stay Israel. Kaifeng carved that permission in stone and set it in the synagogue courtyard.

**Writing it on stone.** Moses told Israel to set up large stones, coat them with plaster and write the words of the law on them once they crossed the Jordan (Deuteronomy 27:2–3). Joshua did it (Joshua 8:32). Joshua also piled twelve stones from the riverbed so that children would ask, "What do these stones mean?" (Joshua 4:6–7). The Kaifeng families set up stelae in 1489, 1512, 1663 and 1679. They were teaching the same question to a Chinese city.

**The scroll that would not stay drowned.** After the flood of 1642 the community gathered what it could and rewrote its Torah scrolls.[^customs] There is an old picture of this in the Hebrew Bible. King Jehoiakim cut up Jeremiah's scroll and burned it in a brazier. Jeremiah simply dictated it again, with more added (Jeremiah 36:23, 27–32). Later, Ezra stood on a wooden platform and read the law aloud to people who had half forgotten it (Nehemiah 8:1–8). Kaifeng lived both scenes: the loss, and the patient copying back.

**How a people gets lost.** This may be Kaifeng's biggest gift. Nobody massacred the Kaifeng Jews. They faded. They married local women. Their bright sons went into government service. Hebrew became a ritual sound and then a mystery. Within a few lifetimes the Sabbath went too.[^customs]

The Bible tells of the northern tribes being carried off by Assyria and settled among the nations (2 Kings 17:6, 23). Hosea gives the sad picture: Ephraim "mixes himself among the peoples" (Hosea 7:8). Then a child is named *Lo-ammi*, "not my people," and in the same breath God promises that where it was said "you are not my people," they will be called "children of the living God" (Hosea 1:9–10).

Kaifeng shows that process in slow motion, with dates. If a community with a synagogue, scrolls and stelae could almost vanish in eight centuries, it is easy to see how tribes taken in the 8th century BC could vanish in twenty-seven. And it shows what lasts longest: a name, a meat rule, a hall of fathers, a family memory that "we are Israel." That is exactly the kind of evidence the Institute looks for among the Qiang, the Karen and the Kyrgyz.

## Where a skeptic will push back

A fair reader will raise several points, and they are strong ones.

First, Kaifeng is not the Ten Tribes. Its people kept rabbinic Judaism, with rabbinic festivals and practice.[^customs] That points to the Jewish communities of Persia and Babylon of the first millennium AD, not to Israelites who left before rabbinic Judaism existed.

Second, the dates. Historians place the arrival in the Song era, about 1,000 years ago. The stelae's claims of Han or Zhou arrival are not accepted; one well-known "Zhou" reading came from a mistranslation by a Western bishop.[^zhou]

Third, the DNA. Preliminary tests on four paternal lines, published by Avotaynu DNA in 2024, found one ordinary East Asian line and others matching Kurdish, Bukharan and perhaps Iraqi Jewish men.[^dna] That fits Silk Road merchants from the Persian world. It says nothing about the Ten Tribes, and the authors make no such claim. These are early findings, not yet peer reviewed.

Fourth, the Hall of the Ancestors may be plain Chinese adaptation, not ancient Israelite memory. Living among Confucians, any minority would build one.

The Institute accepts all of this. Kaifeng is not offered here as a lost tribe. It is offered as a lens. The Bukharan and Kurdish Jews it links to are themselves communities that remember the Assyrian exile. Kaifeng is the end of a long Silk Road chain, and it shows how such chains keep or lose their memory.

## A last word

On the 1489 stele a Chinese emperor tells strangers to honour their fathers' customs. For a while they did. Then, slowly, they forgot almost everything except a name and a cut of meat.

Jacob walked away from the Jabbok limping, with a new name. Some of his children in China walked the same way for eight hundred years. The limp is how you knew them.
