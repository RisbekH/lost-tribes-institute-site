A list of people who have written about the fate of the ten northern tribes of Israel after the Assyrian exile (c. 722 BCE). It includes believers, travelers, missionaries, critics and modern scholars, because the skeptics are part of the story too. Dates are approximate where the record is thin.

## Before the Common Era

**Author of the Book of Tobit** (c. 3rd–2nd century BCE) — Anonymous Jewish writer whose story is set among exiles of the tribe of Naphtali living in Nineveh and Media. It is one of the earliest pictures of northern Israelites living on in Assyrian lands.

## 1st Century CE

**Flavius Josephus** (37–c. 100) — Jewish priest, general and historian. He commanded rebel forces in Galilee during the revolt against Rome, survived the siege of Jotapata, and won his life by predicting that the Roman general Vespasian would become emperor, which he did. Writing later under Roman patronage, Josephus says in *Antiquities of the Jews* (11.133) that the ten tribes were still living beyond the Euphrates in his day as a vast, uncountable population.

**Author of 2 Esdras (4 Ezra)** (late 1st century) — Anonymous Jewish writer after the fall of the Temple. Chapter 13 describes the ten tribes crossing the Euphrates, whose waters were held back for them, and traveling a year and a half to a distant land called Arzareth. Many scholars think the name comes from the Hebrew *eretz acheret*, "another land," in Deuteronomy 29:28. This passage shaped Lost Tribes thinking for centuries.

## 2nd–3rd Centuries

**Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Eliezer** (early 2nd century; recorded in the Mishnah, c. 200) — Leading early rabbis whose debate in Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:3 frames the question: Akiva held the ten tribes would not return, Eliezer held they would. Akiva, who backed the Bar Kokhba revolt, was later executed by the Romans. The Mishnah was compiled under Judah ha-Nasi.

**Commodian** (3rd century) — Latin Christian poet, possibly from North Africa. His *Carmen apologeticum* pictures a hidden nation of the tribes living in peace beyond the known world, who will return in the end times.

## 4th–6th Centuries

**Jerome** (c. 347–420) — Scholar and Bible translator who lived in Bethlehem. In his commentaries on the prophets he notes that the ten tribes were still living in the cities of the Medes under Persian rule.

**Sages of the Babylonian Talmud and Midrash** (compiled c. 400–600) — Rabbinic texts such as Sanhedrin 94a and Genesis Rabbah 73 place the tribes in distant lands, including beyond the legendary Sambatyon River. In the tradition, the river hurls stones six days a week so no one can cross, then rests on the Sabbath, when crossing is forbidden. So the tribes are kept apart until the end of days. Josephus and Pliny the Elder had already written about a "Sabbath river."

## 9th–10th Centuries

**Eldad ha-Dani** (late 9th century) — Traveler who appeared in Kairouan, North Africa, claiming to be from the tribe of Dan. He told of being shipwrecked and captured by cannibals who ate his plump companion, while he was spared for being too thin. He described Dan, Naphtali, Gad and Asher living free in a land beyond Ethiopia, and the sons of Moses beyond the Sambatyon. His customs differed from local Jewish practice, so the puzzled community wrote to Tzemah Gaon, head of the academy in Babylonia, to ask whether to believe him. His account was copied and debated for centuries.

**Hasdai ibn Shaprut** (c. 915–970) — Jewish physician and statesman at the court of Córdoba. When merchants from Khorasan told him of a Jewish kingdom far to the east, he wrote to the king of the Khazars to ask about its origins. The reply attributed to King Joseph traced the Khazars to Togarmah, not to Israel. Even so, the exchange kept alive the question of whether the converted Khazars were connected to lost Israel.

## 12th Century

**Benjamin of Tudela** (traveled c. 1165–1173) — Jewish traveler from Navarre in Spain who spent some eight years crossing the Mediterranean and the Middle East. His *Book of Travels* is still a key source on Jewish life in the 12th century. He reports independent Jewish communities in the mountains near Nishapur in Persia, ruled by their own leader, and Jewish tribes in Arabia around Khaybar. He linked them to Dan, Zebulun, Asher and Naphtali.

**Petachiah of Regensburg** (late 12th century) — German Jewish traveler through Babylonia and Persia. His notes record local traditions about Israelite exiles in the East.

**Author of the Letter of Prester John** (c. 1165) — Anonymous European forgery, addressed to the Byzantine emperor Manuel I, describing a Christian king in Asia whose lands bordered the enclosed ten tribes. Pope Alexander III took it seriously enough to send his physician Philip with a reply in 1177. Philip was never heard from again. The letter spread widely and fed later searches.

## 13th–14th Centuries

**Matthew Paris** (c. 1200–1259) — English monk and chronicler at St Albans. In his *Chronica Majora* he reported the view that the invading Mongols were the ten tribes breaking out of the mountains where Alexander had shut them in. He also recorded a 1241 rumor that European Jews were smuggling weapons to them in wine casks. It shows how the legend could be turned against Jewish communities.

**"Sir John Mandeville"** (c. 1357) — Pen name of the author of one of the most popular books of the Middle Ages, translated into many languages. The author probably never made most of the journeys he describes. The book placed the ten tribes behind the mountains of Gog and Magog in Asia, waiting to come out at the end of the age. Explorers including Columbus knew the book.

## 15th Century

**Obadiah of Bertinoro** (c. 1445–1515) — Italian rabbi and Mishnah commentator who moved to Jerusalem. His letters from 1488 pass on reports heard from travelers about the tribes and the Sambatyon.

## 16th Century

**David Reubeni** (d. c. 1541) — Mysterious adventurer who arrived in Venice in 1523 claiming to be a prince of the tribe of Reuben, sent by his brother, king of a Jewish realm in Arabia. He rode into Rome, was received by Pope Clement VII and the king of Portugal, and proposed a joint army against the Ottomans. He inspired the young Portuguese converso Solomon Molcho, who was later burned at the stake. Reubeni himself died in a Spanish prison.

**Abraham Farissol** (1451–c. 1525) — Italian Jewish geographer whose *Iggeret Orhot Olam* (1524) was among the first Jewish works to discuss the New World. He weighed reports of the tribes, including Reubeni's.

**Diego Durán** (c. 1537–1588) — Dominican friar raised in Mexico. In his *History of the Indies of New Spain* he argued that the Aztecs and other native peoples descended from the Israelites.

**José de Acosta** (1540–1600) — Jesuit naturalist who spent years in Peru and Mexico. He is also credited with one of the first written descriptions of altitude sickness, from crossing the Andes. In *Natural and Moral History of the Indies* (1590) he rejected the Israelite theory for Native Americans and proposed a land route from Asia instead, very close to today's Bering land bridge view. He was an early and influential skeptic whom later Lost Tribes writers had to answer.

## 17th Century

**Gregorio García** (c. 1556–1627) — Dominican friar whose *Origen de los Indios* (1607) surveyed every theory of Native American origins, giving long attention to the ten tribes.

**Antonio de Montezinos (Aaron Levi)** (fl. 1640s) — Portuguese converso traveler. In 1644 he gave sworn testimony to Amsterdam's Jewish community: a native guide had led him deep into the Andes of present-day Colombia/Ecuador, where men came out of the forest reciting the Shema and declaring they were of the tribe of Reuben.

**Menasseh ben Israel** (1604–1657) — Amsterdam rabbi, scholar, and founder of the city's first Hebrew printing press, and a friend of Rembrandt's circle. His *Hope of Israel* (1650) used Montezinos's report to argue the tribes were scattered to the ends of the earth, including America. That scattering, he believed, had to be complete before the redemption. The idea helped drive his 1655 mission to Oliver Cromwell, which opened the way for Jews to resettle in England.

**Thomas Thorowgood** (1595–1669) — English Puritan minister whose *Jews in America* (1650) argued that Native Americans were Israelites, encouraging missionary work in New England.

**Hamon L'Estrange** (1605–1660) — English writer whose *Americans No Jews* (1652) answered Thorowgood point by point.

## 18th Century

**James Adair** (c. 1709–1783) — Irish-born trader who lived some 40 years among the Chickasaw, Cherokee and other southeastern nations and married into a Chickasaw family. His *History of the American Indians* (1775) sets out more than twenty "arguments" from customs, festivals, purity laws and language that he believed proved Israelite descent. It is still valued as an eyewitness record of 18th-century native life.

**Richard Brothers** (1757–1824) — Former British naval officer who proclaimed himself "Prince of the Hebrews" in the 1790s. He claimed that many Britons were hidden descendants of Israel and that he would lead them back to Jerusalem. The government confined him to an asylum in 1795, but his ideas are often seen as a forerunner of British Israelism.

## 19th Century

**Elias Boudinot** (1740–1821) — American statesman, president of the Continental Congress, director of the U.S. Mint, and first president of the American Bible Society. His *A Star in the West* (1816) argued Native Americans were the ten tribes. A young Cherokee man he sponsored took the name Elias Boudinot in his honor and later edited the *Cherokee Phoenix* newspaper.

**Ethan Smith** (1762–1849) — Vermont minister whose *View of the Hebrews* (1823) made the same case for Native Americans and was widely read in New England. Historians have long debated its possible influence on the Book of Mormon, published seven years later not far away.

**William Apess** (1798–1839) — Pequot writer, Methodist minister and Native rights activist, and one of the first Native Americans to publish an autobiography. The appendix to *A Son of the Forest* (1829) argues at length that Native Americans descend from the ten tribes. In his 1831 essay "The Indians: The Ten Lost Tribes" he turned the theory into a demand for justice: if Native people were Israel, how could the nation treat them so badly and not fear the judgment of heaven? He carried the same argument into the Mashpee people's fight for their land in Massachusetts in 1833–34.

**Mordecai Manuel Noah** (1785–1851) — American Jewish journalist, diplomat and playwright. In 1825 he tried to found a Jewish refuge called Ararat on Grand Island in the Niagara River, and invited Native Americans to join as lost Israelites. There weren't enough boats to reach the island, so the dedication was held in a church in Buffalo instead. Ararat never got past its cornerstone. He lectured on Native Americans as the ten tribes in 1837.

**Edward King, Viscount Kingsborough** (1795–1837) — Irish aristocrat who spent his fortune on *Antiquities of Mexico*, lavish volumes of Mesoamerican manuscripts filled with commentary arguing for Israelite origins. The cost ruined him. He was jailed for debt in Dublin and died there of typhus at 42.

**Joseph Wolff** (1795–1862) — German-born Jewish convert and restless missionary traveler. He crossed Persia, Bukhara and Afghanistan looking for the ten tribes and wrote about Bukharan Jews and Afghan traditions of Israelite descent. In 1843 he went back to Bukhara to learn the fate of two executed British officers, Stoddart and Conolly, and narrowly escaped execution himself.

**John Wilson** (1799–1870) — British lecturer whose *Our Israelitish Origin* (1840) laid the groundwork for the British Israel movement.

**Asahel Grant** (1807–1844) — American physician and missionary who worked in the mountains of Kurdistan and Persia. In *The Nestorians; or, The Lost Tribes* (1841) he argued that the Assyrian Christians of the region descended from the ten tribes. He died of typhus in Mosul at 37.

**Francis Mason** (1799–1874) — American Baptist missionary and linguist among the Karen of Burma. He recorded Karen oral traditions of a creator God called Y'wa, a fall from grace, and a lost book that a younger white brother would one day bring back. When missionaries arrived with a book, many Karen saw the tradition fulfilled. Later writers used these traditions in arguing for links between the Karen and ancient Israel.

**J. J. Benjamin ("Benjamin II")** (1818–1864) — Romanian Jewish traveler who styled himself after Benjamin of Tudela. His *Eight Years in Asia and Africa* (1859) describes Jewish communities from Kurdistan to India in the search for the tribes.

**Jacob Saphir** (1822–1886) — Jerusalem rabbi and traveler whose *Even Sapir* (1866) documented the Jews of Yemen, India and elsewhere, noting traditions of tribal origin.

**George Moore** (fl. 1860s) — British physician whose *The Lost Tribes and the Saxons of the East and of the West* (1861) linked the tribes to the Afghans and to the peoples of northern Europe.

**Peter Jones (Kahkewaquonaby)** (1802–1856) — Mississauga Ojibwe chief, Methodist minister and translator from Upper Canada, whose Ojibwe name means "Sacred Feathers." In *History of the Ojebway Indians* (1861), published after his death by his English wife Eliza, he admits he first leaned toward the ten tribes theory after reading Boudinot and Ethan Smith. He granted some likenesses, such as days of purification, first fruits and burnt offerings, but in the end rejected it: his people kept no Sabbath, no circumcision and no clean and unclean animals. He thought an Asian origin more likely, a rare Native voice weighing the theory from the inside.

**Edward Hine** (1825–1891) — English bank clerk turned lecturer whose *Identification of the British Nation with Lost Israel* (1871) made British Israelism a popular movement in Britain and America.

**Nicholas McLeod** (fl. 1870s) — Scottish trader in Japan whose *Epitome of the Ancient History of Japan* (1875) first set out the theory that the Japanese descend from the ten tribes. Among his comparisons was the portable mikoshi shrine, carried on poles at festivals, with the Ark of the Covenant, a comparison still repeated today.

**Henry Walter Bellew** (1834–1892) — British army surgeon and ethnographer in Afghanistan. In *The Races of Afghanistan* (1880) he recorded the Pashtun tradition of descent from the Bani Israel.

**Timothy R. Jenkins** (b. 1821) — Ohio writer of biblical interpretations of history, one of which got him arrested on a charge of blasphemy. His *The Ten Tribes of Israel; or, The True History of the North American Indians* (1883) gathers a huge collection of customs, legends, artifacts and word comparisons from earlier writers to argue that Native Americans are the ten tribes. *The American Israelite*, a Jewish newspaper, praised the collection when it came out, and a 1910 writer noted that Jenkins held the theory without being a Latter-day Saint.

**Andrew J. Blackbird (Mack-aw-de-be-nessy)** (c. 1814–1908) — Odawa (Ottawa) historian, son of an Ottawa chief, and U.S. interpreter in Michigan. His *History of the Ottawa and Chippewa Indians of Michigan* (1887) set down his people's traditions in their own voice and drew parallels with the Bible, including an Ottawa flood story and a tale of a great fish that swallows a famous prophet and casts him out again, much like Jonah.

## 20th Century

**Saeki Yoshirō** (1871–1965) — Japanese scholar of Nestorian Christianity in China. His 1908 writing linked the Hata clan of Uzumasa in Kyoto to Israelite or early Christian origins, and it fed the Japanese–Israelite theory.

**Allen H. Godbey** (1864–1948) — American scholar of Semitic studies at Duke. His massive *The Lost Tribes a Myth* (1930) argued the tribes were never "lost" but absorbed into Jewish communities and local peoples across Asia and Africa.

**Herbert W. Armstrong** (1892–1986) — American founder of the Worldwide Church of God. *The United States and Britain in Prophecy* (1954, later expanded) spread British Israelism to millions of readers and radio listeners.

**Yitzhak Ben-Zvi** (1884–1963) — Historian and second president of Israel. *The Exiled and the Redeemed* (1957) surveyed remote Jewish and Israelite-claiming communities across Asia and Africa.

**Cyrus H. Gordon** (1908–2001) — American scholar of the ancient Near East at Brandeis and NYU. In *Before Columbus* (1971) and other works he argued for ancient Mediterranean, including Hebrew, contact with the Americas.

**Joseph Eidelberg** (1916–1985) — Israeli writer whose *The Japanese and the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel* (1980) compared Japanese and Hebrew words, customs and Shinto practice.

**Don Richardson** (1935–2018) — Canadian missionary and author. *Eternity in Their Hearts* (1981) traces traditions of a creator God and a lost book among the Karen, Kachin, Lahu, Mizo and others. Not strictly a Lost Tribes book, but widely read alongside them.

**Rabbi Eliyahu Avichail** (1932–2022) — Israeli rabbi who founded Amishav ("My People Return") in 1975 to locate descendants of the tribes, traveling to India, Myanmar, Afghanistan and beyond. *The Tribes of Israel* (1990) covers the Bnei Menashe of Mizoram and Manipur, the Pashtuns, the Kashmiris and others. His work helped bring Bnei Menashe immigrants to Israel, and in 2005 Israel's Sephardic Chief Rabbi recognized them as descendants of Israel.

**Dr. Khuplam Milui Lenthang** (20th–21st century) — Kuki ethnographer from Manipur who trained and worked in medicine, then left government service in 1957 to give his life to research. From 1949 he filled diaries with the songs, laments and genealogies recited by Kuki elders, and he wrote some twenty-five books on the origin and culture of the Manmasi (Manasseh) people, including *The Wonderful Genealogical Tales of Manmasi*. His notes turned the skeptical Hillel Halkin around. In one migration lament, a long-tailed wildcat drives the people out of their land, and Halkin recognized the cat as Shalmaneser, the Assyrian king who carried Israel's northern tribes into exile. In 1999 Halkin presented him with a scroll affirming the Kuki, Chin and Mizo as descendants of Manasseh. He met Richard Hewitt (Risbek) for three days in Guwahati in 2006 to compare Kuki and Kyrgyz cultures, and later asked him to write the foreword to his book.

**Zaithanchhungi ("Pi Zaii")** (1939–2017) — Mizo researcher from Aizawl, and in Dr. PC Biaksiama's words the first and foremost modern researcher on the Mizo link to Israel. She was a girls' high school headmistress, a successful life insurance agent, and the author of two travel books. At first she thought the idea absurd and wrote that the Mizo were unlikely to be from Israel. Then, on her first trip to Israel in 1983, Rabbi Eliyahu Avichail challenged her to trace Mizo origins and offered to teach her how to do research. Selling insurance village to village across Mizoram, she asked the elders everywhere what their parents had said about Israel. Their answers convinced her, and her book *Israel–Mizo Identity* sets out the customs and traditions the Mizo share with ancient Israel. A longtime friend of Margot Crossing, who stayed in her home in Aizawl, she researched the subject until her death, and her granddaughter carried the work to completion.

## 21st Century

**Tudor Parfitt** (b. 1944) — British historian, long at SOAS London, sometimes nicknamed a "British Indiana Jones." *The Lost Tribes of Israel: The History of a Myth* (2002) traces how the idea served empire and mission. He is also known for genetic research with the Lemba of southern Africa and for his search for their sacred drum, the ngoma lungundu, which he argued was linked to the Ark of the Covenant.

**Hillel Halkin** (b. 1939) — American-born Israeli writer and translator. He traveled with Rabbi Avichail to northeast India as a skeptic and rejected claim after claim. *Across the Sabbath River* (2002) tells how the notebooks of Dr. Khuplam Milui Lenthang finally changed his mind about the Israelite roots of the Kuki-Chin-Mizo peoples.

**Zvi Ben-Dor Benite** (b. 1966) — Historian at New York University. *The Ten Lost Tribes: A World History* (2009) studies the idea as a force in world history from antiquity to today.

**Shalva Weil** (b. 1949) — Israeli anthropologist at the Hebrew University. She has written widely on the Bene Israel, the Bnei Menashe, the Beta Israel of Ethiopia and Lost Tribes identities.

**Steven M. Collins** (fl. 1990s–2010s) — American author of a series on the ten tribes' migrations through Parthia and Scythia, including *The "Lost" Ten Tribes of Israel… Found!* (1992).

**Yair Davidy** (b. 1948) — Israeli writer and founder of Brit-Am. His books argue the ten tribes are found mainly among the peoples of northwestern Europe and their descendants.

**Arimasa Kubo** (b. 1955) — Japanese Christian writer and speaker whose books argue for Israelite influence on Shinto, Japanese festivals and the Hata clan.

**Margot Crossing** (fl. 2000s–present) — Founder of the Lost Tribes Institute. She lived in Mizoram, northeast India, off and on from 2009 to 2019, getting to know firsthand the Mizo and related peoples who trace their roots to the tribe of Manasseh. She has presented at symposiums in Mizoram, Meghalaya, Assam, Kyrgyzstan, Thailand, Japan, Australia, Vanuatu and Arizona. Her book *From Promise to Restoration* follows the scattering of Israel and its regathering.

**Richard Hewitt (Risbek)** (fl. 1990s–present) — Artist-researcher and author with the Lost Tribes Institute. The grandson of California farmers, he earned a degree in agriculture from Cal Poly San Luis Obispo and lived in Kyrgyzstan from 1994 to 2013 as an agricultural consultant, writer and speaker. There he wrote the Kyrgyz-language book *Боз үй* (*Boz Uy*, "The Yurt") and spoke at symposiums hosted by Kyrgyzstan's Ministry of Culture. With no formal theological training, he is invited across Asia to speak on the Bible's influence on cultures and faiths. His research follows traditions of Israelite descent from Central Asia to Southeast Asia and Japan, among the Kyrgyz, Karbi (Mikir), Karen, Mizo and Japanese. His books include *Come Home* and *Iran's Biblical History*. He mixes scholarly research with art: fiction, video, photography, song and poetry.
