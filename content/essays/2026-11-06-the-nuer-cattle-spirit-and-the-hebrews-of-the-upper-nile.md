Dusk in a dry-season cattle camp on the Sobat River. Smoke from smouldering dung hangs low to keep off the flies. Hundreds of cattle stand tethered to pegs, and young men, their bodies dusted grey with ash, walk among them singing to the oxen they are named after. A man has fallen ill. His kinsmen lead an ox to a peg in front of the hut. An elder steps up with a spear, rubs ash along the animal's back, and speaks to God at length, swinging the spear as he talks. Then he drives it in. The meat is shared out by rule, a leg to this kinsman, a shoulder to that one.

For E. E. Evans-Pritchard, the Oxford anthropologist who first lived in camps like this in 1930, the scene had a familiar ring. In *Nuer Religion* (1956) he wrote that the Nuer "have features which bring to mind the Hebrews of the Old Testament." He added: "When, therefore, I sometimes draw comparisons between Nuer and Hebrew conceptions, it is no mere whim."

This essay takes him at his word.

## Who the Nuer are

The Nuer call themselves Naath, "people." About two million of them live along the Upper Nile in South Sudan and across the border in Ethiopia's Gambella region. They are Nilotic herders, cousins in language and custom of the Dinka, and their life swings with the river. In the rains they live in villages on raised ground. In the dry months they drive the herds down to the water and live in camps.

Cattle carry their whole world. A bride is married with cattle. A killing is settled with cattle. God is approached with cattle. A young man takes a name from his favourite ox and makes songs about its horns and colour.

Sudan's long civil wars, and then South Sudan's own war, cost them dearly. In December 2013, Human Rights Watch reported, soldiers in Juba stopped people, checked faces for the forehead scars that mark many Nuer men, and shot them. Hundreds of thousands fled to the camps of Gambella. Others built new lives in Minnesota and elsewhere in the United States, and beyond.

## Their own origin story

The Nuer tell how they and the Dinka were once brothers, two sons of God. God promised his old cow to Nuer and its calf to Dinka. But Dinka came by night, put on Nuer's voice, and led away the calf. Nuer was left with the old cow, and ever since, the Nuer say, they have raided Dinka herds to get back what was taken.

History adds a second story. In the early 1800s the Nuer pushed east across the Nile, taking land from Dinka and Anuak neighbours until they reached the Ethiopian foothills. It was a people on the move, winning pasture by spear.

And in our own time a third story has been told. In the 1990s some Nuer in Gambella left older Protestant congregations and formed Messianic Jewish communities. They keep Jewish practices and say the Nuer are a lost tribe of Israel. The historian Yotam Gidron, who lived among them for more than a year, has written about them in a Durham thesis (2020), an article in the journal *Africa* (2021) and a Cambridge book, *Nuer Messianic Judaism* (2026).

## The Institute's reading: six mirrors

What follows is the Institute's own reading. These are likenesses of shape, not proofs of blood.

**1. Two brothers, one blessing, a stolen voice.** The Nuer–Dinka story is Jacob and Esau told from the other side of the tent. In Genesis 27 the younger brother comes to his blind father in disguise. Isaac is uneasy and says, "The voice is Jacob's voice, but the hands are Esau's hands" (Genesis 27:22). The blessing goes to the trickster anyway, and the wronged brother swears to take it back (27:41). In both stories the inheritance is livestock and the fertility of herds. In both the feud between the brothers' descendants runs for generations. Edom and Israel fought for centuries. Nuer and Dinka still raid each other's herds.

**2. A man whose name must not die.** When a Nuer man dies without sons, a kinsman marries a wife "to his name." The living man fathers the children, but they count as the dead man's children and carry his line. Anthropologists call it ghost marriage. The Torah has the same instinct. If a man dies childless, his brother marries the widow, "and the firstborn shall carry on his dead brother's name, so that his name is not wiped out of Israel" (Deuteronomy 25:5–6). Boaz marries Ruth "to keep the dead man's name alive on his property" (Ruth 4:10), and Ruth's son is counted to Naomi's family (4:17). Both peoples treat a man's name as something more lasting than his body. A name has to be carried.

**3. A sanctuary for the man with blood on his hands.** A Nuer who has killed someone runs at once to the leopard-skin priest, the *kuaar muon*. In that man's home the killer is safe from revenge. The priest cuts the killer's arm to let the blood out, then goes between the two families until the dead man's kin accept cattle and the feud is closed. The Torah sets up cities of refuge for exactly this moment, "so that a killer may flee there," and keeps him safe from the avenger of blood until his case is heard (Numbers 35:11–12). In both systems blood calls for blood, a sacred person or place stands in the gap, and the community, not the angry kinsman, has the last word. The Institute's essay on the Yibir of Somalia traced the same knot of innocent blood and payment further east on the map.

**4. The ox, the hand, and the poor man's cucumber.** In Nuer sacrifice the animal is tethered, ash is rubbed on its back, God is addressed, and the beast is killed. In Leviticus the worshipper "lays his hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it is accepted for him" (Leviticus 1:4). In both, a person marks the animal as standing for him before it dies. Then there is the cucumber. A Nuer too poor to spare an ox may split a wild cucumber in its place, and the Nuer say, "a cucumber is an ox." Leviticus makes the same allowance for the poor: if you cannot afford a lamb, bring two doves; if not doves, a handful of fine flour (Leviticus 5:7, 11). The rule bends to the size of the herd, and God takes the gift at its intended value.

**5. Tribe, clan, father's house.** Evans-Pritchard is famous for describing how Nuer society splits into nesting segments: tribe, then section, then lineage, then the family around a single hearth. Two lineages that feud with each other join together against a third. When Israel hunts for the man who broke the ban at Jericho, the lot falls first on a tribe, then a clan, then a family, then a man (Joshua 7:14–18). Numbers 1 counts Israel the same way, "by clans and fathers' houses." Both are societies without kings, held together by genealogy and by the knowledge of exactly how far apart any two men stand. The Batak of Sumatra, also on the map, keep the same kind of memory in their *tarombo* genealogies.

**6. Prophets before kings.** The Nuer never had kings. In the 1800s prophets rose instead, above all Ngundeng Bong, who spoke for the divinity Deng, carried a sacred rod, and had his people raise a great mound at Wec Deng. The British bombed the mound in 1928 and took his rod to England. It came back to South Sudan in 2009. Israel in the time of the judges also had no king, and "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). Its leaders were men and women who spoke for God, like Samuel, who went round a circuit of shrines judging the tribes (1 Samuel 7:15–17). And Israel too remembered a holy staff that marked out true authority, Aaron's rod that budded and was kept before God (Numbers 17:8–10).

There are smaller echoes. Jacob blesses his sons with animal names: Judah a lion, Issachar a donkey, Joseph a bull with horns that gore (Genesis 49; Deuteronomy 33:17). Nuer men sing about their namesake oxen in the same spirit. Abraham and Lot split their camps because "the land could not support both of them" (Genesis 13:6), the very logic of a Nuer cattle camp at the end of the dry season. And the cattle peoples elsewhere on the map, the Maasai, the Kalenjin and the Tutsi, show how far this herder's world reaches across East Africa.

## Where a skeptic will push back

The objections are serious and the Institute takes them seriously.

First, likeness is not descent. Levirate marriage, blood money, animal sacrifice and lineage segments turn up among herders all over the world, from Central Asia to the Horn of Africa. Similar lives produce similar laws. Evans-Pritchard himself said the religion most like the Nuer's was that of their Dinka neighbours, not ancient Israel.

Second, the lens. Evans-Pritchard became a Catholic in 1944, more than a decade before *Nuer Religion*. The Oxford anthropologist David Pocock later asked how seriously the book's "approximation to Hebrew monotheism" should be taken. A scholar steeped in the Bible may find biblical shapes because he is looking for them.

Third, the genes. No genetic study has tested the Nuer for Israelite descent. The genomic studies that include them treat them as an old, indigenous Nilotic people of the Upper Nile.

Fourth, the timing. The Nuer lost-tribe claim is recent. Gidron shows that it took shape in Gambella from the 1990s, drawing on what he calls "premillennialist Zionist" ideas, and argues that it recasts a Nuer identity that was always open, able to absorb Dinka captives as full Nuer, into a fixed bloodline. Ironically, that older openness is itself close to the Torah, where Caleb, a Kenizzite, was counted in Judah and became its leader (Numbers 32:12; 34:19).

Fifth, the differences. Nuer religion honours many spirits under one Spirit. Nuer men bear forehead scars, while the Torah forbids cutting the body (Leviticus 19:28).

## Closing

The Nuer have been studied, bombed, displaced, counted and argued over for a century. Whatever their ancestry, they have kept a way of life in which a man's name outlives him, a killer can find shelter, and even a poor man's cucumber is accepted as an ox. Those are old ideas. They are also good ones. The Institute would welcome Nuer voices, in Gambella, in South Sudan and in the diaspora, telling this history in their own words.
