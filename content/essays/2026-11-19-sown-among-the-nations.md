"I will sow them among the peoples, and in far countries they will remember Me" (Zechariah 10:9).

Most readers see Israel's exile in 2 Kings 17 as a punishment and nothing more. A broken people, thrown to the winds, lost. But Zechariah's vocabulary suggests that God does not throw Israel away, but sows Israel into the nations like a farmer sowing seed for a future harvest. That seed grows into the soil it lands in and produces native "fruit of the land."

Think of yogurt. You do not need a gallon of culture to change a gallon of milk. A spoonful does the job. Leave it in the milk overnight, and by morning the whole batch has been changed from the inside. It is still the same gallon of milk, and yet it is totally different.

That is how we at the Lost Tribes Institute read the scattering of Israel. We don't say every Kyrgyz, every Japanese, every Karbi is a descendant of Jacob, but that a seed of Israel, carried into a nation, shaped that nation from within. Its stories, its laws and traditions, its customs and the national memory of one God. Scripture emphasizes this.

## Sown on purpose

Hosea named his son Jezreel, which means "God sows" (Hosea 1:4). The name sounds like a sentence of judgment. Israel's kingdom would fall. But God turns the same name into a promise: "I will sow her for Myself in the land" (Hosea 2:23). The scattering and the sowing are the same word. Judgment was laced with a promise.

Jeremiah says, "I will sow the house of Israel and the house of Judah with the seed of man" (Jeremiah 31:27). And Amos adds that God shakes Israel among all the nations like grain in a sieve, "yet not the smallest kernel will fall to the ground" (Amos 9:9).

God knows where every grain landed. Epigenetics tells us that environment can highlight certain genes and dim other genes. He knows how each Israelite was going to draw on aspects of his/her biblical heritage when cast into foreign lands.

## A little changes the whole

Yeshua told the same story with bread. The Kingdom is like leaven a woman hid in three measures of flour "until all of it was leavened" (Matthew 13:33). She hid it. Nobody saw it work. Yet by the end, the whole batch had risen.

Micah saw Israel the same way. "The remnant of Jacob will be among many peoples like dew from God, like showers on the grass" (Micah 5:7). Dew, coming from somewhere else, seems insignificant and quiet, yet nourishes day after day. Isaiah widened the picture to the whole planet: "Jacob will take root, Israel will blossom and bud, and fill the whole world with fruit" (Isaiah 27:6). Every nation has been influenced by the Holy Script, planted by God inside disobedient Israelites who remembered what they could of their God and biblical customs.

Paul gave the rule behind it. "If the first part of the dough is holy, so is the whole batch" (Romans 11:16). A small, set-apart portion changes the rest. That is the yogurt principle, written in Scripture in the symbolism of the times.

## The promise was always about nations

This was the plan from the start. At Bethel, God told Jacob his seed would "spread out to the west and the east, to the north and the south, and in you and your seed all the families of the earth will be blessed" (Genesis 28:14). Spreading out in every direction was not a failure of the promise. It was the promise.

When Jacob blessed Joseph's sons, he said Ephraim's descendants would become *melo ha-goyim*, "a fullness of nations" (Genesis 48:19). Not one nation. A fullness of them.

The Masoretic text of Deuteronomy 32:8 points out that God had Israel in mind when He divided the peoples and set their borders, "according to the number of the sons of Israel."

## Nations of old

Scripture shows this principle working, one nation at a time.

- **Egypt.** Joseph arrived as a slave. He ended up running the grain supply of the most powerful empire on earth and saving it from famine (Genesis 41). One Israelite, and Egypt was changed.
- **Babylon and Persia.** Daniel was a captive teenager. Before he was done, Nebuchadnezzar had praised the God of Israel in a public decree (Daniel 4), and Darius ordered his whole empire to fear the God of Daniel (Daniel 6:26). The Magi who later came looking for the King of the Jews came out of that same Persian world.
- **Persia again.** Esther and Mordecai turned a plan to wipe out the Jews into a holiday of rescue. As a consequence, "many of the people of the land became Jews" (Esther 8:17), some of them out of fear. Either way, a nation's population shifted because of God's seed. The principle holds.
- **Nineveh.** Jonah did not want to go. He went anyway, preached one short message, and the whole city of Assyria's capital turned, from the king down (Jonah 3).

In every case it was a few Israelites, often captives, often unwilling. The seed was small. The nation was large. And the nation changed.

## Nations of today

The sowing did not stop when the Bible closed. We see the same pattern in living peoples.

- **The Kyrgyz.** Their great epic hero is Manas, son of Jakyb. Manasseh was the son of Jacob. Not every Kyrgyz descends from Manasseh. But the name, the story and the shape of the epic carry a memory of Israel at the heart of the nation. A spoonful of culture in the milk now affects the whole nation.
- **Japan.** Japanese researchers such as Arimasa Kubo point to Shinto shrines laid out like the tabernacle, and to rites that echo from the Torah. To what extent is Japan Israel? I can't answer, but there is amazing research coming from their scholars.
- **Northeast India.** The Karbi (Mikir), with whom the Institute held a symposium in 2024, and the Mizo carry traditions they trace back to Israel. Further south, the Bene Ephraim of Andhra Pradesh claim they descend from the very tribe Jacob called "a fullness of nations" (Genesis 48).

Scholars argue over the details, and they should. But the pattern is the one Zechariah promised. Far from home, in far countries, they remember.

## From "not My people" to children of God

Seed is not sown to stay in the ground. It is sown for a harvest.

Hosea's promise is the harvest: "In the very place where it was said to them, 'You are not My people,' there they will be called 'children of the living God'" (Hosea 1:10). Notice the place. Not back in the land of Israel. Right where they were scattered. The nations that received the seed are the fields where the harvest comes in.

This is why we believe peoples do not need to leave who they are to come home. The seed was planted in their own soil, in their own language, stories and customs. The Anointed One does not ask the Kyrgyz to stop being Kyrgyz or the Japanese to stop being Japanese. He gathers what His Father sowed, first the Jews, then the nations.
