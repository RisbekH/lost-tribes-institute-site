"The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us" (John 1:14). That is the Script's shortest description of the incarnation, and it is also a description of translation. The eternal Word did not stay in a heavenly language that no one could understand. He was born into one family, in one village, speaking one local language. God translated himself into a human life that everyone could understand.

John's word for "made his dwelling" literally means "pitched his tent." In Kyrgyz terms, the Word set up his boz üy among us. That is why I call Isa Mashayak the eternal boz üy, God's living house where heaven meets people. When Chinese translators reached John's opening line, they wrote 太初有道, "In the beginning was the Dao." They did what John himself had done with the Greek word *Logos*: they let the Word wear the deepest word a culture already had.

The historian Andrew Walls made this point decades ago: the incarnation is translation, and every faithful translation since then repeats it on a smaller scale. If that is true, translation is not a side task in the messianic age. It is the way the Messiah greets everyone he meets. For this reason there are more variations of his name than anyone in history.

## The Word Spoke Aramaic

Jesus grew up speaking Aramaic in Galilee and reading Hebrew scripture. Yet his biographies were written in Greek. From the very first generation, the world met Jesus through translation.

The text even shows us the process. Mark keeps a few of his Aramaic words and then translates them on the spot: "Talitha koum," which means "Little girl, I say to you, get up" (Mark 5:41). On the cross: "Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?" which means "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34). The Good Book does not hide that it's a translation.

Before that, Jewish scholars had already translated the Hebrew Scroll into Greek, the Septuagint, in the 3rd century BC. When the Protagonist's message went out to the nations, a translated Script was already waiting for them.

## Babel Reversed, Not Erased

At Babel, God scattered humanity by scattering its languages (Genesis 11). At the festival of Shavuot, after the resurrection, He did not undo Babel by giving everyone one language again. Instead, the crowd from many nations each heard the message "in our own native language" (Acts 2:8).

God did not make the nations learn his language. He spoke theirs. In God's Kingdom, there is no holy language. Instead, each language is integrated into Earth's Heavenly Choir.

The Script's final picture keeps this pattern. John sees a crowd "from every nation, tribe, people and language" standing before the Lamb (Revelation 7:9). The languages are still there at the end. They are not a problem to be fixed. They are part of the honor brought to him.

## No Holy Language

Many religious systems guard one sacred language. Rabbinic Judaism holds Hebrew as the holy tongue, and much of Islam teaches that the Qur'an is only truly the Qur'an in Arabic. A translation is treated as a lesser copy.

The messianic writings take the opposite path. They were written in a language that was not the Messiah's own, and they were translated again almost immediately: into Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ge'ez, Sogdian and many others along the Silk Road. The missiologist Lamin Sanneh called this "translatability." The message is at home in any language because no language owns it. On the contrary, it was created for every language. Every language is holy, because "all things have been created through him and for him" (Colossians 1:16). That includes Japanese, Kyrgyz, Karbi, Mizo and Karen.

## When Translation Stops

History also shows what happens when translation stops. The Septuagint translators turned the Hebrew *Mashiach* into the Greek *Christos*, "Anointed One." That was good translation. But when the Script went into Latin, the translators did not use the Latin word for anointed, *Unctus*. They reshaped the Greek *Christus* (anointed one) as a holy, untouchable word. Over time the whole Latin text came to be treated as too holy to translate into the languages people actually spoke. The light was locked behind a foreign tongue, and the West went into its Dark Ages. It came out as the Book was translated into local languages again.

The East made the same mistake. The Church of the East carried the message across Persia and Central Asia, but its worship stayed in Syriac and did not follow the pattern of Shavuot's tongues of fire. Translation into local languages halted, Isa came to look foreign, and Persia's own hope for a deliverer was cut off from the very Messiah it was waiting for. Even Nebuchadnezzar, Cyrus and Xerxes knew better. They sent sacred words into every language of their empires (Daniel 4:1; Esther 8:9).

The same thing happens today on a smaller scale. When words like Christ, Christian, Bible, and other borrowed religious terms are left untranslated, the Messiah sounds foreign. Even to me as a Western English speaker, "Christ" is a Greek word. "The Anointed One" says what it means.

I watched this happen in Kyrgyzstan in the 1990s. When Kyrgyz read the Injil with his Quranic name, Isa, they told me, "Isa is ours." When they read the same text with the Russian name *Iysus*, they dismissed him as the Russians' foreign god. Missionaries who preferred *Iysus Kristos* over *Isa Mesix* helped turn a nation's love for Isa into suspicion.

To be fair, Christianity has translated the Book into more local languages than anyone, preserving many of those languages and bringing literacy to whole communities. But it often stopped short, leaving titles like Christ and words like baptism and Bible untranslated.

## The Shepherd Speaks Every Tongue

The prophets promised that God would gather Israel's scattered tribes from all the nations (Isaiah 11:12; Ezekiel 34). Those tribes now speak hundreds of languages. A shepherd who can only call his sheep in one language cannot gather a flock scattered across Asia.

This is why the Messiah has collected more names and titles than any other person in history. He is Uzu Masa in Japan, Kydyr Isa to the Kyrgyz, Sot Recho (the Humble King) to the Karbi, and the Peace Child to the Sawi. Native American translators went further and translated the meaning of his name: in *The Great Spirit Walks Among Us*, "Yeshua and his disciples" becomes "Creator Sets Free and the ones who walked the road with him." Each of these titles is a translation. Each one tells a nation that he has arrived in their own house, speaking their own words.

## Every Reader Translates

Translation does not stop when the Script reaches our language. It continues in every reader's mind. When we read about the Pharisees, we don't picture first-century teachers in Judea. We picture the powerful, hypocritical leaders of our own day. When we read about Rome, we see the empire of our time. Without noticing, we are translating the whole Script into our own setting as we read.

Yeshua did this himself. He read Isaiah in the Nazareth synagogue, rolled up the scroll, and said, "Today this scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). An ancient text became a present event.

But inner translation needs the same care as written translation. The historical Pharisees were respected teachers, and some, like Nicodemus and Gamaliel, were open to Jesus. His warning was aimed at hypocrisy, not at a social group. A faithful reader casts himself in the story too, not only his opponents. The Pharisee we are warned about may be the one in the mirror.

## Conclusion

No incarnation without translation. The Word became flesh once, in Galilee, in Aramaic. In the messianic age, he becomes Japanese, Kyrgyz, Persian and Karen through every faithful translation of his name, his words and his story. A Messiah who can only be met in a foreign language has not yet been fully incarnated into that nation.

So the work is simple and never finished: we must constantly translate.
