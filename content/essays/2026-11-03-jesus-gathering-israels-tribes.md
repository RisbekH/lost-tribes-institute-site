## The Shepherd and the Scattered Flock

The Hebrew prophets gave Israel a clear sign by which to know the Messiah: he is the shepherd through whom God gathers His lost sheep. Ezekiel paints this picture more fully than any other prophet. The rabbis took Ezekiel’s word and turned it into a test. Maimonides, Judaism's great teacher of law, wrote that whoever gathers the dispersed of Israel "is certainly the Messiah."

This essay takes the rabbis at their word. It sets out Ezekiel's picture, then the sages' test, and then asks who has actually done this work. The answer is Jesus, Yeshua, or whatever you call him in your nation. He called himself the good shepherd and sent his first followers to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel." When they asked Jesus when Israel would be restored, he did not dismiss the hope. He sent them to the ends of the earth, where the lost tribes had gone.

The evidence is not only in old texts like Ezekiel, written 600 years before Jesus. We’ve also found that peoples across Asia recall an Israelite origin after hearing the message of Jesus, which ultimately puts Humanity’s Script (Bible) in their hands and heart. That Script includes the Torah. In the Torah they recognize their own songs, laws and customs, and recognize Israel as their ancient home. By the rabbis' own measure, this is the Messiah's work.

## Ezekiel 34: God Goes Looking for His Sheep

Ezekiel 34 begins with a charge against Israel's leaders. They fed themselves and let the flock go hungry. God lists what they failed to do: "You haven't strengthened the weak, healed the sick, or bandaged the injured. You haven't brought back the strays, and you haven't searched for the lost" (34:4). The result was exile: "My sheep were scattered over the whole face of the earth, and no one searched or looked for them" (34:6).

God expected those who associate with His name to be engaged in finding His sheep. Then God makes a promise. He will do the searching Himself: "I myself will search for my sheep and look after them. As a shepherd looks for his scattered flock, so I will look for my sheep. I will rescue them from every place where they were scattered on a cloudy and dark day" (34:11–12). He goes on: "I will look for the lost, bring back the strays, bandage the injured, and strengthen the sick" (34:16).

The chapter's turning point comes when God says His gathering will be performed through one man: "I will place over them one shepherd, my servant David, and he will feed them. He will be their shepherd. I, the LORD, will be their God, and my servant David will be prince among them" (34:23–24). David had been dead for nearly four hundred years when Ezekiel wrote this. The prophet is not speaking of the old king. He is speaking of a coming son of David, the anointed one, the Messiah.

Two things stand out. First, the work is God's own work, yet it is carried out by a human shepherd from David's line. Second, the shepherd's job is defined by the flock's condition. They are lost, scattered and far away. A Messiah who did not go looking for them would not be the shepherd.

## Ezekiel 36: Gathered, Then Changed from Within

Ezekiel 36 tells us what the gathering is for. God brings his people back for the sake of his own name, which their exile had disgraced among the nations (36:22–23). Then comes the promise: "I will take you from among the nations, gather you out of all the countries, and bring you into your own land" (36:24).

But the return is not just a change of address. God continues: "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you. I will take away your heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh. I will put my Spirit within you and cause you to walk in my laws, and you will keep my rules and do them" (36:26–27).

God gathers, then cleanses, then puts His Spirit inside, and the Spirit brings people back to his laws. The law is not forced on them from outside. They are moved from within to keep it, as if God’s values had become their values.

Ezekiel 37 ties chapters 34 and 36 together. God tells the prophet to take two sticks, one for Judah (the southern kingdom) and one for Joseph, which stands for Ephraim and the northern tribes. These two divided nations become one in his hand (37:16–19). "I will take the children of Israel from among the nations where they have gone, and gather them from every side" (37:21). And again the Davidic shepherd appears: "My servant David will be king over them, and they will all have one shepherd. They will walk in my rules and keep my laws" (37:24). The two become one under the shepherd’s reign.

So Ezekiel's full picture has four parts: one shepherd from David's line, the scattered tribes found and gathered, the two houses of Israel made one, and a people moved by God's Spirit back to his laws. These form a pattern this essay will trace: Jesus’ global gathering of peoples who responded to Jesus’ life, death, and ultimate victory over the grave.

## What the Rabbis Said: The Messiah Is Known by the Gathering

Jewish teachers read Ezekiel the same way. For them, gathering the exiles is not a side task of the Messiah. It is one of the primary signs by which he is recognized.

**Maimonides (Rambam, 1135–1204)**, the most influential Jewish legal scholar of the Middle Ages, wrote the standard description of the Messiah in his *Mishneh Torah*, Laws of Kings, chapters 11 and 12:

> "The King Messiah will arise in the future and restore the kingdom of David to its first power. He will build the Temple and gather the dispersed of Israel." (Laws of Kings 11:1)

He then gives the test for knowing him:

> "If he does this and succeeds, builds the Temple in its place, and gathers the dispersed of Israel, then he is certainly the Messiah." (Laws of Kings 11:4)

Maimonides rejected Jesus as the Messiah. Yet he also admitted that through Jesus "the whole world has become filled" with the Torah and the hope of the Messiah, reaching "the furthermost islands." He thought this only prepared the way. But by Maimonides’ own standard, the work he credited to Jesus is the Messiah’s work.

**The Mishnah (about 200 CE)** records a debate about the ten northern tribes that Assyria carried east (2 Kings 17). Rabbi Akiva read Deuteronomy 29:28, "he cast them into another land, as it is this day," to mean that just as a day passes and does not return, the ten tribes would never return. This is the same Rabbi Akiva who later backed Bar Kokhba’s failed revolt against Rome. **Rabbi Eliezer** disagreed: "As this day: just as the day grows dark and then grows light again, so it will be for the ten tribes. Though it has grown dark for them, it will grow light for them in the future" (Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:3).

The Talmud sides strongly with Rabbi Eliezer. **Rabbi Yohanan** said that in this ruling "Rabbi Akiva abandoned his kindness," and pointed to Jeremiah 3:12, where God calls toward the north, "Return, faithless Israel... I will not be angry forever" (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 110b). Many later authorities follow Rabbi Eliezer: the ten tribes will be reunited with Judah in the days of the Messiah.

The same Rabbi Yohanan said how great that day will be: **"The day of the ingathering of the exiles is as great as the day on which heaven and earth were created"** (Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 88a). He drew this from Hosea 1:11, where the children of Judah and the children of Israel are gathered together under one head, "for great will be the day of Jezreel." Jezreel means "God sows." Hosea uses the name first for judgment, when Israel is scattered like seed (1:4–5), and then for restoration: "I will sow her for myself in the land" (2:23).

The hope of Israel’s return is prayed every day. The tenth blessing of the Amidah, the central Jewish prayer, asks God to "sound the great shofar for our freedom, raise a banner to gather our exiles, and gather us together from the four corners of the earth," echoing Isaiah 11:12, where God "will gather the outcasts of Israel and collect the scattered of Judah from the four corners of the earth." This is God sowing them back into Israel (Jezreel).

The rabbis, therefore, agree with Ezekiel. The anointed son of David is the one who gathers. Whoever finds and brings home the lost of Israel, including the tribes everyone else had given up on, is distinguished as the Messiah. The question is who has done it.

## Jesus: "I Am the Good Shepherd"

Jesus did not leave his listeners to guess which role he claimed. His words and actions follow Ezekiel's shepherd step by step. He saw a scattered flock, sent helpers after it, took the shepherd's title for himself, and made gathering the test of loyalty to him.

### He saw a scattered flock

When Jesus looked at the crowds, "he was moved with compassion for them, because they were harassed and scattered, like sheep without a shepherd" (Matthew 9:36). That is Ezekiel 34:5 come to life: "they were scattered because there was no shepherd."

His answer was to choose twelve men, matching the twelve tribes, and send them out with clear orders:

> "Don't go among the Gentiles, and don't enter any town of the Samaritans. Instead, go to the lost sheep of the house of Israel." (Matthew 10:5–6)

Jesus claimed the Messiah's mission when he described his own calling: "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 15:24). And he summed up his purpose in Ezekiel's words: "The Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10; compare Ezekiel 34:16, "I will seek the lost").

He even promised his twelve disciples that when all things are renewed, they would sit on twelve thrones "judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matthew 19:28). Jesus never spoke as if ten of those tribes were gone for good. Like Rabbi Eliezer, he knew light would return to them.

### "I am the good shepherd"

Jesus claimed the shepherd's title outright:

> "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." (John 10:11)

> "I am the good shepherd. I know my own, and my own know me." (John 10:14)

The leaders listening would have heard Ezekiel 34 behind these words. Ezekiel set the bad shepherds who fed themselves against the one shepherd God would raise up. Jesus set himself against the hired hand who runs when the wolf comes (John 10:12–13). Religious leaders throughout the ages have turned on Jesus for this stark condemnation. Jesus described his own mission in Ezekiel's terms:

> "I have other sheep, which are not of this fold. I must bring them also, and they will hear my voice. They will become one flock with one shepherd." (John 10:16)

"One shepherd" is the exact promise of Ezekiel 34:23 and 37:24. Jesus is claiming to be the Davidic shepherd who makes the scattered flock one again.

### "Whoever does not gather with me scatters"

Jesus used Ezekiel's two key verbs to draw a line around himself:

> "Whoever is not with me is against me, and whoever does not gather with me scatters." (Matthew 12:30; also Luke 11:23)

In Ezekiel, *scattering* is what happened to Israel under judgment and bad leaders: "I scattered them among the nations, and they were dispersed through the countries" (36:19); "they were scattered because there was no shepherd" (34:5). *Gathering* is what God promises to do through his shepherd: "I will bring them out from the peoples, gather them from the countries, and bring them into their own land" (34:13); "I will gather you out of all the countries" (36:24).

So when Jesus says that gathering happens *with him*, he places himself where Ezekiel placed the shepherd. He is the center around whom Israel is regathered. To stand apart from him is to keep doing the work of the bad shepherds, scattering the flock.

History gave a painful example. About a century after Jesus, Rabbi Akiva refused to gather with Jesus and hailed the rebel leader Simon Bar Kokhba as Messiah instead. When Akiva saw him, he declared, “This is the King Messiah.” Another sage, Rabbi Yohanan ben Torta, answered, “Akiva, grass will grow from your cheeks, and the son of David will still not have come” (Jerusalem Talmud, Ta’anit 4:5). Bar Kokhba’s followers turned on the followers of Jesus. Justin Martyr, writing at the time, says Bar Kokhba ordered them punished unless they denied Jesus (*First Apology* 31).

The revolt (132–135 CE) ended in disaster. The Roman historian Cassius Dio reports hundreds of thousands of Jews killed and nearly a thousand villages destroyed. Jews were banned from Jerusalem, and Judea was renamed to erase its memory. This is exactly what Ezekiel said false shepherds do to God’s flock. They were slaughtered and scattered.

### Gathering through his death

John shows that even the death of Jesus served this purpose. When the high priest Caiaphas said one man should die for the nation, John notes that he spoke as a prophet without knowing it: Jesus would die "not for the nation only, but to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad" (John 11:51–52).

*Gather* and *scattered* appear in one sentence. The death that seemed to end his mission was, in John's view, the start of Ezekiel's gathering. What Jesus said after his resurrection shows how that ingathering would go on.

## Acts 1: "Will You Now Restore the Kingdom to Israel?"

After his resurrection, Jesus spent forty days teaching his followers about the kingdom of God. Their last question to him was the natural one for Jews who knew Ezekiel’s book:

> "Lord, are you now restoring the kingdom to Israel?" (Acts 1:6)

They expected the restoration, but hadn’t yet seen it. Many readers assume Jesus scolded them, as if hoping for Israel's restoration were a mistake. He did not. He never said the kingdom would not be restored, or that Israel no longer mattered. He set aside only the question of timing:

> "It isn't for you to know the times or seasons that the Father has set by his own authority. But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth." (Acts 1:7–8)

Read this answer with Ezekiel in mind and it fits the pattern closely. **First**, the Spirit comes just as Ezekiel 36:27 promised, "I will put my Spirit within you." Jesus tells them the restoration will move forward by that same Spirit.

**Second, the route runs through Samaria.** Samaria was the heartland of the old northern kingdom, the land of Ephraim and Manasseh. In Jesus' day the Samaritans still claimed descent from those tribes. Jesus sends his witnesses to the very region the lost tribes were taken from, and then beyond it.

**Third, "the ends of the earth" is where the scattered had gone.** The phrase echoes Isaiah 49:6, where God says to his servant: "And he said to me, It is a great thing for you to be called my servant, to establish the tribes of Jacob, and to recover the dispersed of Israel: Look, I have given you for the covenant of a race, for a light of the nations, that you should be for salvation to the end of the earth" (Septuagint). Raising up the tribes of Jacob and reaching the ends of the earth belong to one mission. Paul quotes this same verse as his own commission (Acts 13:47).

In Matthew, the charge is the same: "Go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19). Earlier the disciples were told not to go to the nations. Now they are told to go to all of them. The shift is not away from the lost sheep of Israel. It follows them from Jerusalem. Those sheep had been "scattered over the whole face of the earth" (Ezekiel 34:6), so the shepherd's gatherers had to go to the whole earth.

The apostles understood it this way. At Pentecost, the first crowd to hear them included "Parthians, Medes, Elamites, and residents of Mesopotamia" (Acts 2:9), the same lands where Assyria had settled the northern tribes, in "the cities of the Medes" (2 Kings 17:6). Paul told King Agrippa he was on trial "for the hope of the promise made by God to our fathers, which our twelve tribes hope to attain" (Acts 26:6–7). James addressed his letter "to the twelve tribes scattered abroad" (James 1:1), and Peter wrote to those "living as foreigners in the dispersion" (1 Peter 1:1). Peter also said that heaven would hold Jesus "until the times of the restoration of all things, which God spoke of by the mouth of his holy prophets" (Acts 3:21).

### The shepherd works through his body

Someone may object that Jesus left the earth before the gathering was finished. But the New Testament does not picture him as absent. It pictures him as working through his people, who are called his body.

Luke opens the book of Acts by calling his first book an account of "all that Jesus began to do and to teach" (Acts 1:1). Acts is the story of what Jesus went on doing through his followers. When Saul was hunting down those followers, the risen Jesus stopped him with the words, "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" (Acts 9:4). To strike his people was to strike him.

Paul made this the center of his teaching:

> "Now you are the body of Messiah, and each of you is a part of it." (1 Corinthians 12:27)

> "Just as the body is one and has many parts, and all the parts, though many, are one body, so it is with Messiah. For by one Spirit we were all immersed into one body, whether Jews or Greeks." (1 Corinthians 12:12–13)

> "He is the head of the body, the assembly." (Colossians 1:18)

Jesus himself set up this pattern before he left. "As the Father has sent me, so I send you" (John 20:21). "Whoever believes in me will do the works that I do, and greater works than these, because I am going to the Father" (John 14:12). And his last promise in Matthew comes right after the command to go to all nations: "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20).

He also passed his shepherd's work to them in so many words. To Peter he said three times, "Feed my lambs... Shepherd my sheep... Feed my sheep" (John 21:15–17). Peter later told other leaders, "Shepherd the flock of God that is among you," not for gain but willingly, "and when the Chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the crown of glory" (1 Peter 5:2–4). This is Ezekiel 34 turned right side up. The bad shepherds fed themselves. The shepherds Jesus appoints feed the flock, and answer to him.

Peter could then write to the scattered believers of Asia Minor: "You were going astray like sheep, but now you have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls" (1 Peter 2:25).

So the gathering did not stop when Jesus rose to the Father. The shepherd kept searching for his sheep through the hands, feet and voices of his people. Every person who carried scripture to the hills of Burma or northeast India was, in the New Testament's own terms, the body of the shepherd still at work.

The restoration of Israel was not cancelled. It was put in motion, and the disciples were sent out to carry it to the places where the lost had gone. If that is true, we should be able to see the results.

## The Peoples Who Found Their Root Through Jesus

Jesus said his sheep would "hear my voice" (John 10:16). Across Asia, that is what happened. Again and again, a people who held an old memory of coming from a distant land first met the message of Jesus. That message brought them scripture (the Bible), where they encountered the Torah and found their own customs written there, sometimes in surprising detail. The path ran in the order Ezekiel 36 describes: gathered to the shepherd, given the Spirit, and led back to God's laws. At Lost Tribes Institute (LostTribesInstitute.com) we have written about many of these nations. Many of the contributors are Asians presenting at symposiums on the topic, including the Kuki, Mizo, Chin, and Karbi (Mikir) of northeast India, the Karen of Myanmar and Thailand, Japanese, Kashmiris, Kyrgyz, and Afghans. Their paths differ. Some, like the Karen and Mizo, came to the Torah through faith in Jesus. Others keep Israelite memories within other faiths, waiting for the shepherd's voice.

Scholars debate these Israelite descent claims, and DNA studies have not settled the issue. Some of these communities, such as Bnei Menashe and the Bene Ephraim, later moved from faith in Jesus to rabbinic Judaism. But the order of events remains striking. In almost every case, the first step was faith in Jesus. It was the shepherd's voice that brought the Torah to these peoples, and then the Spirit highlighted the Torah to show them who they were.

## By the Rabbis' Own Measure

The sages did not leave the Messiah undefined. Maimonides gave a checklist, and its heart is the gathering of the dispersed of Israel and the restoring of the tribes. Measured against that list, Jesus is the one figure in history who has done what the rabbis said the Messiah would do.

| --- | --- | --- |
| A son of David, the one shepherd | Ezekiel 34:23; Maimonides, Kings 11:1 | Called son of David from his birth to his entry into Jerusalem (Matthew 1:1; 21:9); "I am the good shepherd" (John 10:11) |
| Gathers the dispersed of Israel | Maimonides, Kings 11:1, 11:4 | Sent his followers to the lost sheep of Israel, then to the ends of the earth where they were scattered (Matthew 10:6; Acts 1:8) |

| Brings the nations to serve God together | Maimonides, Kings 11:4 | His followers carried the God of Israel to every nation (Matthew 28:19) |

### Answering the strongest objection

Maimonides himself raised the strongest objection. He wrote that if a claimant does not succeed, or is killed, he is not the Messiah, and in the same chapter he named Jesus as such a failed claimant. A serious argument must face this directly. Three answers come from Jewish sources themselves.

**The Talmud knows of a Messiah who is killed.** In Sukkah 52a, the sages explain Zechariah 12:10, "they will look on me whom they pierced, and mourn for him as one mourns for an only son," as mourning for *Messiah son of Joseph*, who will be slain. Jewish tradition kept room for a Messiah of Joseph's line, the line of Ephraim and the lost tribes, who dies. A slain Messiah is not foreign to the rabbis. It is in the Talmud. Interestingly, the New Testament teasingly records Jesus being called the son of Joseph (Luke 3:23 and John 6:42).

**The prophets describe an anointed one who is cut off.** Daniel 9:26 says, "after the sixty-two weeks the anointed one will be cut off…, then the people of the prince will come and destroy the city and the sanctuary." Sure enough, the sanctuary fell in 70 CE, about 40 years after Jesus was “cut off.” Isaiah 53 speaks of a servant "cut off from the land of the living" who still "will see his offspring" and "prolong his days" (53:8, 10). The followers of Jesus said this is exactly what they saw: he died and rose to see God’s children continuing his work.

**The gathering is a process, and Ezekiel says so.** Ezekiel 37 shows dry bones coming together step by step: first a rattling, then bone to bone, then flesh, and only last the breath. Isaiah 27:12 says the children of Israel will be gathered "one by one." The rabbis themselves compared Israel's redemption to the dawn, which breaks little by little (Jerusalem Talmud, Berakhot 1:1). In Sanhedrin 98a, Rabbi Joshua ben Levi is told that the Messiah sits among the suffering poor at the gate of the city, binding their wounds one at a time, ready to come "today, if you will hear his voice" (Psalm 95:7). A Messiah who gathers his sheep one by one, across centuries, through the voice of his people, fits the sages' own pictures.

What of the Temple? Jesus said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," and John adds that "he spoke of the temple of his body" (John 2:19–21). Ezekiel's own promise of the gathered nation ends the same way: "I will set my sanctuary among them forever. My dwelling place will be with them" (37:26–27). Paul applies the same picture to the people of Jesus: "Don't you know that you are a temple of God, and that God's Spirit lives in you?" (1 Corinthians 3:16). Those gathered to him are "being built together into a dwelling place of God in the Spirit" (Ephesians 2:22). In this sense the Messiah is already building God's house, out of the very people he gathers.

### Conclusion

Ezekiel said God would search for his scattered sheep himself and set one shepherd, a son of David, over them. The rabbis said this shepherd would be known by his gathering of the dispersed, even the ten tribes that others had given up for lost. Jesus called himself the good shepherd, said that whoever does not gather with him scatters, sent his followers first to the lost sheep of Israel, and then, when asked about Israel's restoration, sent them to the ends of the earth where those sheep had gone.

Two thousand years later, peoples from the hills of Myanmar to the mountains of northeast India heard his voice, opened the Torah, and found their own story there. By the sages' own measure, that is the work of the Messiah. Every day Israel prays, "Gather us together from the four corners of the earth." That prayer has an answer in Jesus.
