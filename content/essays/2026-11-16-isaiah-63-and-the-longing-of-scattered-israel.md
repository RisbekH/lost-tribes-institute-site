## Isaiah 63: a people cut off from their own lineage

Isaiah 63:7 to 64:12 is a communal lament, and its voice is a people who have been away so long they're no longer sure they belong. In their anguish, they remember the loving-kindness of their ancient God. In verse 16 the people address God, "But you are our Father, though Abraham does not know us or Israel acknowledge us; you, Lord, are our Father, our Redeemer from of old is your name."

That sounds like people cut off from their own lineage, holding on to God when they can no longer hold on to their family tree. The Jews of Judah's southern kingdom, distinct from Israel, were still in their land. The northern tribes of Israel had already been exiled. Then 63:19 says, "We have become like those you never ruled, like those not called by your name." They were no longer distinct from the nations they lived among.

God's response comes after the lament, in 65:1: "I was found by those who did not seek me... to a nation that was not called by my name, I said, 'Here I am, here I am.'" The lament says "we've become like people not called by your name," and the answer is that God reaches those exact people.

Paul applies this verse to the nations (Gentiles) in Romans 10:20, suggesting that those who are not God's people (Gentiles) are the object of his love. In other words, the line between scattered Israel and the nations is blurred.

## How much of Isaiah carries this longing

A great deal of it. Chapters 40 to 66, about 27 of the book's 66 chapters, mostly speak to people in exile or just after it. The gathering theme also runs through the first half.

| Passage | What it says |
| --- | --- |
| 11:11–16 | A "second time" recovery of the remnant from Assyria, Egypt, Elam, Shinar, Hamath and "the islands of the sea." Names both "the outcasts of Israel" and "the dispersed of Judah," and says the rivalry between Ephraim and Judah will end. |
| 27:12–13 | A great trumpet calls home those "perishing in the land of Assyria," where the northern tribes were taken. |
| 43:5–7 | "Bring my sons from afar and my daughters from the ends of the earth." |
| 44:5 | "One will say, 'I am the LORD's'; another will call himself by the name of Jacob." People taking on the name, not necessarily born into it. |
| 49:12–15 | Some come "from the land of Sinim," which many have read as China. Zion says "the LORD has forgotten me"; God answers that a mother might forget her nursing child, but he will not forget. |
| 56:8 | "I will gather yet others to him besides those already gathered." |
| 66:19–20 | Messengers go to Tarshish, Pul, Lud, Tubal, Javan and "the distant coastlands," and bring the brothers home. |

## The other prophets

The theme of God's unrelenting desire to reclaim his people isn't limited to Isaiah. In some prophets it's the main storyline.

- **Hosea** is the prophet of the northern kingdom, and the most personal. The people are named *Lo-ammi*, "not my people," yet "in the place where it was said, 'You are not my people,' they will be called children of the living God" (1:10). "Israel is swallowed up; now they are among the nations" (8:8). "How can I give you up, Ephraim?" (11:8).
- **Jeremiah** calls toward the north: "Return, faithless Israel" (3:12). His chapters of comfort (30–33) include Rachel weeping for her children and Ephraim grieving over himself. God answers, "Is Ephraim my dear son?... I will surely have mercy on him" (31:15–20). The return from "the land of the north" will outshine the Exodus from Egypt (16:14–15; 23:7–8).
- **Ezekiel:** the dry bones say, "Our bones are dried up, our hope is lost" (37:11), the same voice as Isaiah 63. The two sticks of Judah and Joseph become one (37:15–22). God himself searches for the scattered sheep (ch. 34). He has been "a little sanctuary" to them in the countries where they went (11:16).
- **Amos 9:9:** Israel is shaken among the nations like grain in a sieve, "yet not a kernel will fall to the ground."
- **Micah 7:14–20** almost mirrors Isaiah 63. It asks God to shepherd his flock "as in the days of old," and ends with faithfulness to Jacob and loyal love to Abraham "as you swore to our fathers from days of old."
- **Zechariah 10:9:** "Though I scattered them among the peoples, yet in far countries they will remember me." This far country seems to be the setting of Israel's cry in Isaiah 63:7 through 64:12.

Behind all of them stands Deuteronomy 30:1–4. When you "call these things to mind among all the nations" where you have been driven, God will gather you "even from the ends of heaven." The prophets are largely working out God's promise to return all the tribes.

## Where it leads

The longing carries into the New Testament after the Servant in Isaiah 49:6 was sent "to restore the preserved of Israel," and also to be a light to the nations, the Messiah says he was sent "to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matt. 15:24). He also speaks of "other sheep not of this fold" (John 10:16). He was obviously talking about the nations. John adds that Jesus would die to "gather into one the scattered children of God" (John 11:52), a reference to the scattered tribes of Israel. James writes "to the twelve tribes scattered abroad" (James 1:1). The prophets voiced the yearning, and the New Testament presents the Anointed One as the answer.

The longing of scattered Israel for the love of their ancient God is a major theme in scripture. Jesus' fulfillment of this worldwide longing often is not addressed in the context of Israel's lostness.
