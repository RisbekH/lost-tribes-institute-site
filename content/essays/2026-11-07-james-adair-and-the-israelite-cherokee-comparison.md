## Introduction

In the eighteenth century, knowledge of Native North America came from people who lived there, not from theory. Few Europeans knew the Southeastern nations as well as **James Adair** (c. 1709–1783). His title page calls him "a trader with the Indians, and resident in their country for forty years." He traded first with the Catawba and Cherokee and later lived mostly among the Chickasaw, and he knew the Choctaw and Muskogee (Creek) as well. His stated aim was "to give the Literati proper and good materials for tracing the origin of the American Indians." He learned their languages, sat in their councils, and shared their dangers, hunger, diplomacy and wars.

From those years Adair reached a conclusion that is still controversial: that the Cherokee and their neighbors descended from the **ancient Israelites**, the people often called the Lost Tribes. He did not get there by deferring to scholars in Europe. He got there by comparing what he saw, day after day, with what he knew from the Hebrew Bible, Josephus and the classical writers. To Adair the Bible was a history of a real people with real institutions. If another people kept the same institutions, he thought history, not coincidence, was the best explanation.

This essay does not try to settle his conclusion. It lays out **why Adair believed what he believed**, and why his comparisons, taken together, looked so strong to a man who treated biblical history as true history. His case was cumulative: tribes and clans, religion, ritual, purity laws, the calendar, warfare, language and memory. One likeness proves little. Dozens of them, across unrelated parts of life, were too many for Adair to call accidental.

Adair's book opens with twenty-three numbered "Arguments." The 1775 table of contents lists them in this order:

## Adair's evidence, point by point

The notes below summarize what Adair reports under these headings and elsewhere in the book, with the Bible passages that come to mind. They follow my research notes rather than Adair's exact order. My own Kyrgyz comparisons are marked as mine.

**Division into tribes.** The Indians divide into tribes or clans, such as the eagle, panther, buffalo and bear, each with its badge or emblem, much like the twelve tribes of Israel. Leaders mark treaties with their tribal symbols. Adair stresses that they do not worship these animals. *My comparison:* the Kyrgyz also keep tribal names and emblems. They sacrifice a lamb to God and divide it into twelve parts for the "twelve Kyrgyz tribes," though there are far more than twelve Kyrgyz tribes today. Is the memory of the original twelve tribes kept in that old sacrifice?

**Worship of one God.** Adair reports that the Cherokee, Muskogee, Choctaw and Chickasaw worshiped a supreme being whose name he wrote Yo-He-Wah, which he took for the Hebrew YHWH. In his words, "they do not pay the least perceivable adoration to any images, or to dead persons; neither to the celestial luminaries, nor evil spirits, nor any created being whatsoever." He also writes that "they pay no religious worship to stocks, or stones, after the manner of the old eastern pagans; neither do they worship any kind of images whatsoever." They honored a holy fire that the chief priest renewed each year. God appeared to Moses in fire (Exodus 3) and called himself a consuming fire (Deuteronomy 4).

*My comparison:* this fire-centered belief in one God has kin among the Altai of Siberia, relatives of the Kyrgyz. The old Kyrgyz name for God, EGE, is a long way from YHWH ("He is," Exodus 3:15) but not far from EHYEH ("I am," Exodus 3:14):

> But Moses said to God (ELOHIM), "Suppose I go to the Children of Israel and say to them, 'The God (ELOHIM) of your fathers has sent me to you,' and they ask me, 'What is His Name?' What should I say to them?" God (ELOHIM) answered Moses, "I AM WHO I AM" (EHYEH ASHER EHYEH). Then He said, "You are to say to the Children of Israel, 'I AM' (EHYEH) has sent me to you." God (ELOHIM) also said to Moses: "You are to say to the Children of Israel, HE IS (YHWH), the God (ELOHIM) of your fathers, the God (ELOHIM) of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My Name forever, and the Name by which I should be remembered from generation to generation." (Exodus 3:13–15)

The Kyrgyz have another old name for God, BARBARDIGER, which means something like "the One who is, who is," keeping the meaning of the name given to the Children of Israel thousands of years ago. Scripture's account of God scattering the Children of Israel among all nations is true, and so are the prophecies that those nations will come back to him.

**A theocracy.** They saw the Deity as the direct head of their state and called themselves "the beloved people," close to Israel's "chosen people." Adair describes an elder urging the people to keep "the old, beloved speech" and praising "their beloved land that flowed with milk and honey."

**Angels.** They believed in good spirits that help the virtuous and in evil beings, which they called "accursed people" or "accursed beings," living in the dark regions of the west. Compare Acts 12 and Job 33.

**Language.** Adair thought their languages resembled Hebrew in structure: short, emphatic and musical, with few prepositions, few pronouns and no comparatives. He connects their word for a prophet, built on *cheera*, "fire," with the Hebrew *nebia*. Mooney and later linguists rejected his word comparisons, and readers should weigh this argument lightly.

**Counting time.** They counted time by moons and named months after fruits and seasons.

**Prophets and high priests.** They had prophets and high priests in a ranked order, with a holy place set apart. The priesthood passed to the eldest son, and lame men were once barred from serving (compare Leviticus 21:16–23).

**Festivals and fasts.** Adair describes a yearly atonement with no work; a feast with booths of green branches and songs of "Halelu-Yah"; the green-corn dances; and fasts of two nights with bitter drinks. Compare Leviticus 23, Exodus 29 and Numbers 15.

**Daily sacrifice and offerings.** At meals they threw a piece of fat meat into the fire. Venison was passed through fire to take away the blood before it was eaten, and purification for unknown faults led to forgiveness.

**Purity laws.** Temple purity was strict. Women in their monthly period and after childbirth lived apart, and those who touched the dead were unclean until purified. They held that they could perform "their religious ablutions and purifications, in any deep clean water."

**Abstinence from unclean things.** They avoided blood, swine and reptiles, and took no salt or flesh during sacred festivals for up to four days.

**Cities of refuge.** Each nation kept "either a house or town of refuge." For the Cherokee it was Chota; for the Muskogee, the upper town of Koosah. Blood was answered with blood "unless in some very particular case when the eldest can redeem." Compare Numbers 35 and the kinsman-redeemer of Ruth.

**Sanctuary and sacred fire.** They sang and danced in three circles around their sacred fire, which Adair connects to Hebrew practice. Holy places faced west.

**Divine names in song.** Besides Yo-He-Wah and Halelu-Yah, Adair heard what he took for Elohim, Shiloh and Messiah in their chants. These are the weakest of his comparisons, since similar syllables are common in Native song.

**First fruits.** The new fruits were made holy by the chief priest after the harvest and eaten only after the offering. Compare Deuteronomy 26.

**Counting.** They counted by tens and by moons, and Adair compares some number words to Hebrew and Syriac usage.

**Traditions and testimony.** They had oral traditions from their forefathers about spirits and laws, which Adair saw as a faint image of the Hebrew oral law. He gathered Spanish, Peruvian and Mexican reports as well, and compared their westward migration stories and the sameness of their rites across many nations.

## Adair on Native morality, honesty and virtue

Adair writes as a man who knew the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw and Muskogee for four decades. He sees them as a moral people living under an old theocratic code, and he often sets their virtues against the corruption of European traders.

**Moral foundation.** Most of their rules, he says, "are derived from the plain law of nature." The chief priest urged the people "to imitate the noble actions of their great and virtuous forefathers." Disease and famine were seen as punishment for broken laws, such as impurity or adultery.

**Honesty.** Among themselves, he writes, they are "honest and harmless to each other," though he adds that this "may be through fear of resentment and reprisal." Their oath, as he translates it, means "Do not you lie? Do you not, of a certain truth?" The Chickasaw and Choctaw call a deceitful person *seente*, "a snake," and say a liar has "the snake's tongue." Toward Europeans, Adair paraphrases, they could be crafty, and he blames much of that on traders who cheated them.

**Hospitality and generosity.** Generosity was a standing virtue. "To be narrow-hearted, especially to those in want, or to any of their own family, is accounted" a crime. A Cherokee saying about a stingy man was "The great hawk is at home." Adair praises the old Muskogee Wolf-King, at whose house travelers found friendship.

**Liberty.** They had no despots. Chiefs led by merit, persuasion and courage, and every warrior guarded his own honor.

**Endurance.** They lived plain lives, fasted before war and rites, and were "almost as impenetrable to cold, as a bar of steel." Adair blames European liquor for much of the damage done to them.

**Courage.** He tells of warriors who, facing death, "fought as became desperate men, deprived of hope." He writes that the bravest warriors were usually the most humane to prisoners (my paraphrase).

**Justice.** Murder was avenged with blood, and Adair speaks of a time "when equal blood has not been shed to quench the crying blood of their relations." Towns of refuge sheltered the man who killed by accident. Of the Choctaw he says they "are the most formal in their addresses, of all the Indian nations I am acquainted with."

**Character.** His summary is frank: "They are ingenious, witty, cunning, and deceitful; very faithful indeed to their own tribes," they "never forget injuries," and they are "revengeful of blood, to a degree of distraction." They also possess "a strong comprehensive judgment." Adair does not idealize them, but he sees their ethics as a noble remnant of something very old.

## What the Jews lost, the Lost Tribes kept, and what Joseph lost, the Jews kept

Joseph's descendants, known as Israel's lost or scattered tribes, lost the Torah itself but kept something of its practices after they were deported. Judah's descendants, the Jews, lost Moses' Ark but kept the blueprint, the Torah.

These two brothers and their descendants represent the divided inheritance of Israel. About three thousand years ago Israel split in a civil war. Judah in the south kept the written Torah, the genealogies and the record of the covenant (Genesis 49:10; Romans 3:1–2). Joseph in the north, called Israel or Ephraim, led the ten northern tribes, and according to Jeremiah, God divorced them in the eighth century BC. They were scattered among the nations and, as promised, became "a multitude in the midst of the earth" (Genesis 48:19). Today we call them the Lost Tribes of Israel.

Judah guarded the text. Joseph's tribes lost the book, but some of them, seeing their exile as punishment, held on to what they could. So their cultures are like archaeological sites, with new customs built over older "ruins." The lower layer shows their former Torah practice.

One of the clearest examples is the sacred chest, reminiscent of the Ark of the Covenant, found among peoples linked with the Lost Tribes. These chests are not copies made from the pages of the Torah, but they are recognizable, like offspring of the Ark carried down for nearly three thousand years. Far from Jerusalem, they still bear Mosaic fingerprints.

### The Ark as the center of the covenant

The Ark was more than a wooden box. It was the center of Israel's relationship with God. Exodus 25:10–22 describes a gold-covered wooden chest carried on poles, guarded by strict purity laws, holding the tablets of the covenant and covered by the cherubim. It was the throne of God's presence, the meeting place between God and Israel, set in the Holy of Holies, first in the Tent and later in the Temple.

Treating it wrongly brought disaster. Israel lost to the Philistines (1 Samuel 4), and Uzzah died for touching it (2 Samuel 6:6–7). Impurity, not military weakness, decided the outcome. Judah kept this theology in Scripture and worship. Joseph's descendants kept the Ark's holiness in other forms, spread across several nations.

### The Native American "ark"

Adair described a small, square wooden box, easy to carry, that went with the warriors to war. It was treated as very holy and never set on the ground; it rested on stones or logs. Warriors fasted, washed and kept purity laws so as not to defile what Adair called their "holy ark." A defeat was blamed on impurity, not poor tactics, which is the covenant logic of Joshua 7 and 8. After a victory, peace offerings were made with joy only if the warriors were clean, echoing Leviticus 7:11–21.

These tribes did not keep Torah scrolls. They kept a distant trace of a Torah culture: judgment for impurity, victory through obedience, and a memory of God's presence tied to a sacred chest.

### The Kyrgyz *sandyk*

Among the Kyrgyz and the related Altai peoples, a sacred chest called a *sandyk* stands in the place of honor in the home, the spot furthest from the door, just as the Ark stood at the far end of the Tabernacle and later the Temple. It is often covered with metalwork and patterns that could evoke angels, and it is treated with more reverence than any ordinary household object.

Here again a piece of the covenant survives. I believe the Kyrgyz descend from Israel's northern tribes. Their hero "Manas son of Jakyb" is, in my judgment, the "Manasseh son of Jacob" of Genesis 48:5. Their chest is not the Ark of gold and cherubim described in Scripture, but an ancestral memory, kept in the place of highest honor.

### Japan's *omikoshi*

Japanese Shinto keeps another Ark-like object, the *omikoshi*, a portable shrine carried at festivals. The Japanese researcher Arimasa Kubo has noted these parallels:

- Both are carried on poles on the shoulders (1 Chronicles 15:15).
- Both are box-shaped, often gold-covered, and treated as the dwelling of a deity.
- Both are carried with music, dancing, shouting and celebration, like David bringing the Ark to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6).
- The bearers purify themselves and wear white, like the Levites.

The deity is no longer called YHWH, but the picture is clear. To me it shows that Israelites reached as far as Japan and took pieces of the old covenant with them, which makes it likely such pieces exist among all nations, as God promised in Amos 9:9: "For surely I will command, and will sift the house of Israel among all nations, as grain is sifted in a sieve."

### A short comparison

| Culture or object | Key features and parallels |
|---|---|
| Biblical Ark (Exodus 25) | Gold-covered acacia box, shoulder poles, cherubim and mercy seat, God's meeting place; strict purity rules; defeats blamed on defilement |
| Native American (Adair) | Small portable wooden box, never set on the ground, purity and fasting required; defeats blamed on impurity. The strongest parallel in function |
| Kyrgyz and Altai *sandyk* | Sacred chest in the place of honor furthest from the door, ornate metal covering, patterns that may evoke angels |
| Japanese *omikoshi* (Kubo) | Gold-like portable shrine on shoulder poles, processions with song and dance, purification of the bearers. Strong visual parallels, but used at festivals rather than in war |

### Judah kept the text; Ephraim kept the pattern

The same exercise works for many other Torah fragments. Many nations tell of a lost book. Others keep clean and unclean foods. Many in Asia say they descend from the tribe of Manasseh, others recall an origin in the Middle East, many wear fringes, and some use the horns of a bull, the emblem of Joseph's tribes.

Judah lost the Ark where God's presence dwelt but kept the written blueprint. Joseph lost the text but still keeps something of the old Torah. These are not pagan inventions. They are broken continuities, changed by time, distance and mixing, but kept for a purpose.

Across continents and centuries the Ark keeps reappearing, not whole and not necessarily with God's presence, but as a witness to God's hand in long migrations. These altered arks are one example of many Torah practices kept among God's former children, who have been kneaded into the nations like yeast into dough. They are road markers pointing to a day when the Messiah will bring them home to the presence of God. As Jeremiah said on God's behalf, "Set up road signs; put up guideposts. Take note of the highway, the road that you take. Return, Virgin Israel, return to your towns" (Jeremiah 31:21).
