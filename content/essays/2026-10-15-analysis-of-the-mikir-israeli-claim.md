## Introduction

The Lost Tribes Institute (LTI) was invited to analyze ethnographic data that highlights ethnic Mikir, also called Karbi, descent from ancient Israel. After reviewing the information, the LTI concurs that ethnic Mikir/Karbi people, estimated to be 500,000 (India Times; 2024), are descendants of ancient Israel's Northern Kingdom, which, according to the Hebrew Prophets, was scattered among all nations (Amos 9:9), and which the Prophets say will return to Israel (Jeremiah 31:10). Archaeological evidence from the Middle East confirms the diaspora described in the Script, and recent anthropological studies in Eurasia and beyond affirm the modern-day existence of Israel's non-Jewish, Israelite descendants. Note: the Northern Tribes were not from Judah’s Southern Kingdom, from where modern Jews derive their name.

## Challenges and Considerations

### Evidence Gaps

Insufficient genetic evidence linking Mikir to ancient Israel challenges the claim of direct descent. Mitochondrial DNA links a small percentage of women to the Middle East, but to date, the male Y-chromosomal link is virtually absent. This has been a problem in our field and in Judaic studies for decades. Genetics is

not as straightforward as we had originally imagined but will be worked out in time.

Central Asians have legends of a group of women being the sole survivors of an attack. By sticking together, the women restarted the tribe. This legend, if true, would explain why Middle Eastern mitochondrial DNA is present, but not Middle Eastern Y-Chromosomal DNA.

Nonetheless, an unusual Jewish genetic marker called G6PD <br>
(Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) is prevalent among Southeast Asia’s Hill Tribes. Both Mediterranean Jews and Pushtuns have this marker, confirming the evidence for an Israelite connection to the Pushtuns and the Hill Tribes. A study among the Kachin Jingpo showed a prevalence of 27.9% in males and 30.6% in females on the Myanmar-Yunnan border (Li Q, 2015). Mukherjee MB (2015) also records the prevalence of G6PD among India’s North Eastern Hill Tribes. LTI’s own Margot Crossing has three Mizo grandsons from Mizoram. Two have G6PD.

## Cultural and Religious Parallels

### Monotheism

The Mikir are often considered polytheistic but have a deep-seated and historical monotheistic faith called Honghari, which sets them apart from the surrounding polytheistic cultures. Their monotheism helped Mikir/Karbi preserve their faith, identity, and culture through centuries of oppression and servitude. Their spiritual adherence matches Thomas Torrance’s descriptions of Qiang-Min in Sichuan, China. Torrance argues that the Qiang-min are Israelites. Similarly, the Mikir legend and the legend of other similar tribes in NE India acknowledge at least one migration from Sichuan.

"Hempu" is the most common title for the Almighty in the Mikir/Karbi tradition, but the Moserah (the oral history of Mikir/Karbi) references two powers, Ji and Hova. These names appear to derive from "Jihova" or "Jehova," a variation of the Hebrew name for God (Migration Memories, p. 8). Another Mikir name for God is "Yahwek." The Lost Tribes of Israel (LTI) research often finds variations of "Yah" or "Yahweh" among the scattered tribes of Israel. According to the Moserah, Mikir lived alongside the children of Ji and Hova. Ethnographers generally agree that Ji and Hova's people are Jews. These two deities might represent the plurality often used to address the One God in many monotheistic traditions. (Recently, Mikir have adopted Hinduism and Christianity which Mikir/Karbi clans consider foreign.)

Mikir monotheism may have come from Zoroastrianism or the Hindu Vedas which originated as monotheistic. Additionally, the Lost Tribes Institute also sees evidence that both Zaroastor and the later Vedas were also influenced by Israelites and/or Jews. The Mikir/Karbi core is strongly monotheistic and this is indicative of an Israelite origin.

## The Three Religions of Karbi/Mikir Tribes

Mikir Christians argue that their parents and grandparents received Christianity because scripture paralleled their traditions and appeared to be a type of national restoration. Unfortunately, Mikir Christians are now abandoning their scriptural traditions for a Western form of Christianity.

Hindu Vedas affirm Hinduism’s foundational belief in One Creator God, but Mikir converts to Hinduism probably converted because of Hinduism’s proximity to and influence over Mikir populations, a pattern that exists at the edges of other minority populations. Unfortunately, our research has not yet included Karbi Hindus.

Honghari, the indigenous folk faith, is often considered animistic, which suggests that Mikir/Karbi believe objects have a spirit. Ancient Israel also mixed monotheism with the worship of neighboring gods, some of whom were gods of nature’s various elements (mountains, valleys, fire). That may sound counterintuitive. They believed in the Almighty but adopted other gods and religious practices from foreign sources. Despite all this, Mikir disavow idol worship.

## Written Text

The Lost Tribes stretching north and south along the Hindu Kush-Himalayan region from Myanmar to Kyrgyzstan have stories of a Lost Book. Among the Mikir, we heard stories of different lost texts. The most common version was a sacred text that was written on tree bark. The prized bark-book was eaten by the people during a famine. Longsing Bey, a traditional teller of the Moserah, confirmed that their text had been written on bark and stones (December 12, 2024). The Mikir Hills have various trees including banana trees with smooth bark which may have depicted their ancient script. “Kitab Lo” is another name of the lost book, referenced in Sonoram Rongphar’s research paper. “Kitab Lo” is simply two different words for “book”. Kitab is a Semitic word for writing or book that stems from the Hebraic consonantal word, K-T-B.

East of Nowgong District, Mikir speak of a book that was written on buffalo-skin scrolls which were eaten by rats which were sent by demonic forces who wanted to keep the Mikir people ignorant (UMRS p. 4). To this day, Mikir, who educate themselves, fear a dark curse. This curse on a nation’s intellect parallels a depiction in the Kyrgyz Epic, Manas, which depicts the enemy burning the Kyrgyz scroll to keep Kyrgyz minds darkened (Жакыпбеков p. 12). Again, Mikir not only trace their origins back to Israel but fit within a network of nations with Israelite roots.

## Blood Sacrifices

Mikir, like many indigenous tribes, offer blood sacrifices to both their monotheistic God, Hempu, and also to lesser deities. Goat and fowl sacrifices are most common, but, contrary to the Script, pigs are also sacrificed. Animals are sacrificed to appease Hempu for blessings on the crops and for protection. Pig sacrifices are the antithesis of God’s commands, but such contrary data doesn’t exclude Mikir from being Israelite. Even the Script highlights Israel’s northern tribes eating pork 2800 years ago (Isaiah 65:2-4).

Across the Mikir regions, different clans report applying sacrificial blood to doorposts, a practice that mirrors ancient Israel’s Passover (Exodus 12 & 13). This practice has also been noted among ethnic Karen in northern Myanmar and past generations of ethnic Kyrgyz.

Blood is also sprinkled on the small earthen altars that Mikir shovel together during a ceremonious dance. Sprinkling blood on the altar is a common practice of ancient Israelites (Leviticus 1:5). The Mikir altar is not very impressive, but serves as a temporary site for ceremonial sacrifices.

Two permanent altars in Mikir lands were mentioned. One needs to be cleared each year for an annual festival. The other site has since come under Kashi ownership and is no longer used by Mikir tribes.

## Drink Sacrifices

Mikir pour water and other drink offerings on their dirt altars. The Script also contains similar drink offerings (Nothengpi, Karbi Heritage Museum, December 13, 2024).

Like ancient Israelites, Mikir have priests who must purify themselves and wear priestly garments during ceremonies. And like both Israelites and Kyrgyz, they use incense in their ceremonies for purification.

The Mikir clan “Kathar” is the priestly clan I met while among the Mikir. Israel’s priests are called Kohen. The H in Kohen can become TH through phonetic aspiration, and phonetically the N can become R through Rhotacism. Phonetically, the consonantal K-H-N of Kohen can become K-TH-R of Kathar. ## Cultural Narratives

### Machir, son of Manasseh

Christian Mikir sources point out the similarities between the ancient tribal name Mikir and the Script’s Machir clan which is recorded as one of Manasseh’s clans. Manasseh is significant for our research in Asia because the majority of “Lost” Israelites we’ve worked with in Asia identify as descendants of Manasseh. These same nations are closely related to Mikir/Karbi. The related tribes (Kuki, Mizo, and Chin) are accepted by modern Israel as ancient descendants of Israel’s Tribe, Manassah. Additionally, scholars like Dr. Avigdor Shachan, who quotes numerous sources, highlight the 2700-year preponderance of evidence highlighting Manasseh’s tribe in Central Asia and beyond. Jewish enclaves in Kranganur, Kerala also claim descent from Manasseh’s tribe. The Manassites and Nephtelites are the most documented Israelite tribes in Asia.

Mikir are part of a fascinating network of Asian tribes, including a distant connection with modern Kyrgyz who have a well-documented history stemming from Manasseh’s Tribe. The Kyrgyz hero, Manas son of Jakyb, appears to be the Script’s “Manasseh son of Jacob” (Hewitt, 2012). The Kyrgyz epic Manas mentions kin who live on the other side of the Himalayas in thick jungles with tigers, monkeys, and thieves. The comparative analysis with Mikir (Karbi) and other groups with Israelite affinities to ancient Israelite customs fits into the larger body of evidence confirming a strong Israelite presence throughout Asia.

## Twelve Leaders

Multiple sources confirmed that Mikir have a creation story with a giant extinct bird that laid 12 eggs which hatched into the twelve original leaders of the Mikir. One source referenced these as 12 brothers (UMRS, p. 7-8). This is significant since ancient Israel formed around the 12 sons of Jacob (Israel). These 12 brothers became the leaders of Israel’s 12 tribes.

According to tradition, Mikir land has also been divided into 12 portions even though there are only 5 Mikir tribes. Under Joshua, Israel’s land was divided into 12 tribal territories (Joshua 15-19). Likewise, Kyrgyz divide a lamb’s carcass into 12 parts in remembrance of their 12 original tribes. Today, Kyrgyz have more than 12 tribes.

## Oral History - Moserah

The Mikir “Moserah” is an oral retelling of Mikir history, customs, and migrations. Their Moserah narrative may take its name from the Script’s Moserah, one of Israel's campsites on their migration from Egypt to Canaan. On that journey, the Israelites were commanded to repeat their history to their children. Moserah is the place where Israel’s High Priest died, and the priesthood passed to the next generation. Likewise, Moserah would have been the place where one Israelite generation recited their history, practices, and migrations to the next generation.

Kyrgyz have an oral history that is recited in an identical rhythm to the Moserah’s slow version which Longsing Bey recited on December 12, 2024, in Plasa, Meghalaya. The similarities with Kyrgyz and other Israelite tribes support the data that Mikir/Karbi are within the network of Asia’s ancient Israelite communities.

The Mikir Moserah mentions crossing an Ocean like Israel’s Red Sea crossing. Additionally, the Moserah reminds Mikir that they originated from the “Navel of the World” or the “Center of Earth” which is a colloquial definition of “Zion”.

Oral history and linguistic analysis strongly suggest one migration came through Yunnan and Burma. Karen, Karbi/Mikir, and Kuki-Chin all belong to the Tibeto-Burman branch of the Sino-Tibetan linguistic family (Post, pp. 213–242), but these tribes all mention Shinlong, the caves high above Chengdu. Sinlong or a variation of that name is missing from the Mikir migration narratives.

Other sources say the Mikir came from Punjab or over the Himalayas (Barua (pp. 23–25). The Vedas’ use of “Kirat” for Indo-Mongoloids who have therefore been in the subcontinent since the Later Vedic Period which concluded circa 500 BC.

Karbi megaliths also suggest an early migration. Additionally, oral history indicates the Mikir were influenced by Buddhism from Tibet and/or Nepal. The Moserah which suggests Mikir came through Tibet and Nepal, where Buddhism thrived. And some scholars suggest Mikir ancestors came from Bhutan. Obviously, there was not one migration.

The Kyrgyz epic “Manas” mentioned earlier suggests trade, communication, and aid passed back and forth through and around the Himalayas. Bhutan and Northern Assam, directly north of the Mikir Hills, name a river and park “Manas” in a region full of thick jungles and tigers. Bhutan is referenced as one migration site in Mikir oral tradition, so we can assume descendants of Manasseh’s Tribe from Machir’s Clan passed this way also. Mikir/Karbi appear to be a conglomeration of various migrations through mountain passes that are still accessed in summer months by pilgrims and hikers.

## Sot Recho

From Kyrgyzstan to Japan, and from Siberia to India, we have discovered that ethnic groups with connections to ancient Israelites share a peculiar belief in a messianic figure within their customs and faith. The Mikir culture exemplifies this intriguing pattern. The Mikir believe in a Humble King, known as Sot Recho, who was rejected by his people, killed, yet remained unkillable. He is prophesied to return (Rongphar, 2024), aligning with scriptural depictions of a Messiah who was killed and promises to return.

Although the messianic figures vary across cultures, the Script’s Jesus, whom Mikir call Jizu, fits every typology we have examined, from the Peace Child of Papua New Guinea to Shunu of Altai, Sot Recho of Mikir, the Wandering Kydyr of Kyrgyzstan, and many more.

One explanation for this messianic phenomenon can be attributed to Jesus’ edict to his followers to go to the “lost sheep of Israel” which is the Prophet Ezekiel’s metaphor for Israel’s scattered tribes (Ezekiel 36). A handful of scholars believe Jesus’ followers proclaimed Jesus' story and teachings to their scattered kin during the first millennium AD.
