A professor in Hyderabad named Manasseh wrote a history of India's religions.  Prof. P. Manasseh (Manasseh Pasupuleti) published [*A Historical Overview of the Indian Religions*](/library?q=Historical%20Overview%20of%20the%20Indian%20Religions) in 2000. It reached a seventh edition in 2016, and an Indian seminary uses it as a textbook. It is short and blunt: 34 brief chapters that run from the seals of the Indus Valley to the Sangh Parivar of today. It reads less like a theology book and more like a field guide to how ideas travel.

## His big claim: India's faith survives by absorbing

Manasseh's starting point will surprise many Western readers. "Hinduism," he says, is not one religion. It is hundreds of religions held together by two things: caste and karma. Everything else is open to change. A new god, a new teacher, a new idea arrives, and before long it has a place in the family. He sums it up in one line: "While syncretism is a weakness of most religions, it is the strength of Hinduism."

Buddha became an avatar of Vishnu. Jain teachers became forms of Narayana. Then Manasseh asks the question that runs through the whole book: what else came in from outside?

## The Semitic thread

His answer starts much earlier than most people expect.

**Varuna and the God of Abraham.** An early Vedic hymn begs Varuna to remove the singer's wrongs and not to punish him "for the guilt of others." Manasseh notes that the Indologist A.L. Basham also wondered whether Varuna owes some of his character to Semitic influence.

**Keturah's sons.** Genesis says Abraham sent the sons of Keturah away "eastward, to the east country" (Genesis 25:6). One of them was Ishbak. Manasseh sets him beside the Ikshvaku, the ancient royal line of India that Rama himself belongs to.

**Israelites on the Konkan coast.** In his chapter on Judaism in India, Manasseh cites the Scottish scholar John Wilson. In 1838, Wilson told the Royal Asiatic Society in Bombay that the Bene Israel belonged to the lost Ten Tribes. Their neighbors called them Shanwar Telis, the "Saturday oil-men," because they rested on the seventh day.

**Persia's fingerprints.** Cyrus and Darius ruled the Indus country. Manasseh says the sacred thread and the forehead mark began in Zoroastrian practice. In the old rite, the mark was the blood of a sacrifice. This is the same Persian empire that held Israel's northern tribes in its eastern provinces and also held India's northwest door.

## Thomas, coconut and betel leaf

Manasseh's longest argument is about Jesus. He holds that Thomas, one of Jesus' closest followers, came first to Taxila, where coins of the king Gondophares still turn up, and then to Kerala in 52 CE. He says the early followers there remembered Jesus' last meal with coconut, and in the north with betel leaf. Later, the poet Kabir put coconut and betel into his own blessed-food offering.

From there he follows attributes of Jesus, one after another, into Indian thought:

- **God taking on flesh.** He argues that the avatar idea is missing from the Vedas and the Upanishads, and that it grew strong only after Jesus.
- **Devotion instead of ritual.** Bhakti, love for one personal God open to every caste, rose against the Brahmin priests' monopoly on sacrifice.
- **A three-in-one God.** Vallabha taught God as the Supreme Person, the Word, and the Spirit who lives within.
- **The Spirit as wind.** Madhva made Vayu, the wind god, the go-between for God and people. The Hebrew ruach and the Greek pneuma both mean wind and spirit.
- **A rider on a white horse.** Kalki, the last avatar, comes on a white horse to end the dark age. Manasseh sets him beside Jesus’ return on the white horse (Revelation 19:11).
- **A god who forgives.** Venkateshwara, whose name Manasseh reads as "the God who forgives sins," brought an idea of sin that he says older Hindu teachers had rejected.

## A Hindu who saw Jesus as one of India's own

The most moving page belongs to Keshab Chandra Sen, a 19th-century Brahmo Samaj leader. He told Indian audiences that Jesus comes to them "as an Asiatic in race, as a Hindu in faith, as a kinsman and brother." Sen did not ask Hindus to stop being Indian. He told them that in Jesus they would find the fulfillment of their own scriptures and prophets.

That is the same thread I followed in my book, India's Most Popular Man. When a Hindu priest once told me that Hindus love Yeshu and that his teaching fits inside their faith, he was standing in a long line that Keshab Chandra Sen had already walked.

## Reading Manasseh with Israel's lost tribes in mind

Manasseh is not writing about the Ten Tribes. Still, his book keeps walking across this ground.

**The timing.** Assyria carried Israel's northern tribes into exile between 740 and 721 BCE and settled them "in the cities of the Medes" (2 Kings 17:6). Within two centuries, northern India saw what Manasseh calls the revolt against Vedic ritual: Mahavira, the Buddha, the Upanishad teachers and the Ajivikas. All of them turned against priests who demanded animal sacrifice for every favor. Manasseh credits India's warrior class. We ask a further question. Did some of these ideas arrive with exiles who already remembered a God who wanted mercy more than sacrifice?

**Israel is not the same as Judaism.** Manasseh keeps two groups apart. The Jews of Kerala came as traders. The Bene Israel are the ones Wilson tied to the Ten Tribes. That is the line the Institute draws every day. The scattered tribes show up as customs, not as rabbis and synagogues: a seventh-day rest, oil in their rites, and Hebrew place names worn down into Marathi surnames such as Kehimkar and Penkar.

**The name on the cover.** Manasseh is the tribe whose name surfaces across Asia. There is Manas, son of Jakyb, in the Kyrgyz epic, Manasia in Mizo chants, and Manmasi among the Kuki-Chin. Manasseh notes that many tribal peoples of Northeast India turned to Jesus. That is the same region where the Institute held its first scientific symposium, in Aizawl in 2023.

**Jesus on the exiles' roads.** Manasseh says Thomas chose Kerala partly because a sizable Jewish community already lived there. Jesus' followers walked roads that Israel's scattered tribes had walked first. When Indians took up Jesus' teaching, they did it in Indian ways: coconut, betel leaf and bhakti songs. They did not trade their culture for someone else's. That is the pattern the Institute keeps finding among Jesus-centered communities of every faith. The same ancient oracles, read in many languages, keep calling scattered kin home.

## Where a skeptic will push back

This book has a point of view, and a careful reader should see where it stretches.

- **Dating.** Most scholars place devotion to Krishna and Vasudeva before Thomas. The Heliodorus pillar at Vidisha, put up by a Greek ambassador more than a century before Jesus, honors Vasudeva. The Bhagavad Gita's verses about God coming "age after age" are usually dated before the Common Era too. Manasseh's claim that the avatar idea grew up only after Jesus is a minority view.
- **Sound-alikes.** Ishbak and Ikshvaku sound alike, and that isn’t always considered proof that they were the same.
- **His stance.** Manasseh openly criticizes the caste system and the Sangh Parivar, and he says so plainly. Readers on the other side of those debates will read these chapters differently.

He also admits that the borrowing went both ways. Indian thought, he says, shaped the Stoics and the Gnostics. In general, Professor P. Manasseh’s work aligns with what the Lost Tribes Institute sees across Asia. We’re grateful for his contribution and honored to share his work.
