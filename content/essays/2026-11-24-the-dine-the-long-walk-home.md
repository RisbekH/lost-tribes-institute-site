A young veteran comes home to the high desert. He has been to a war on the other side of the world, and he has seen the dead. His family does not throw him a party first. They call a singer. For several days and nights there are songs, a painted staff carried from one camp to another, and a blackening of his body. The point is plain to everyone there: he has touched the enemy dead, and he must be made clean before he can live among his own people again.

This is the Enemy Way, one of the great ceremonies of the Diné, the people most Americans call the Navajo. Families still hold it for their soldiers.

Anyone who has read the Book of Numbers will feel a jolt of recognition.

## Who the Diné are

The Diné hold the largest reservation in the United States, spread across Arizona, New Mexico and Utah. Their language belongs to the Athabaskan family, whose other branches are spoken in Alaska and western Canada. They are shepherds, weavers, silversmiths and singers. A Diné person still introduces himself by four clans: born to his mother's clan, born for his father's, then the clans of his mother's father and his father's father. Marrying inside your own clan is forbidden. There are many clans, and the first ones, the Diné say, were made by Changing Woman.

Their homeland is a shape, not just a place. Four sacred mountains mark its edges: Blanca Peak in the east, Mount Taylor in the south, the San Francisco Peaks in the west and Hesperus Peak in the north. Inside them is home. Outside them is danger.

## The story they tell

The Diné do not say they came from Israel. They say they emerged.

In their own account the people climbed up through a series of lower worlds. In one of them a great flood rose, and they escaped by climbing up through a hollow reed into the world above. In this world, Changing Woman gave birth to twin sons, Monster Slayer and Born for Water. The twins went to their father, the Sun, were tested, and came back armed to kill the giants and monsters that preyed on the people. After that the land was made safe to live in, and the Holy People taught the ceremonies, the hogan and the right way to walk in hózhó, which means beauty, balance and harmony all at once.

The Israelite reading of the Diné came from outside. In the 20th century, Latter-day Saint missions taught that Native Americans were Lamanites, descendants of Lehi of the tribe of Joseph. Some Diné took that identity to heart. The best known, George P. Lee, became the first Native American in the movement's general leadership in 1975. In 1989 he broke with its leaders, and he was expelled that year. His life shows both how deep the idea went and how much it could cost.

The Institute does not rest on the Book of Mormon. We look at the customs themselves and ask what they look like beside the Hebrew Bible.

## Reading the customs beside the Hebrew Bible

What follows is the Institute's reading. These are connections we see, not facts we can prove.

**Warriors made clean.** After the war with Midian, Moses tells the fighting men to stay outside the camp for seven days. Anyone who killed or touched the dead had to purify himself on the third day and the seventh. They washed their clothes before they came back in (Numbers 31:19–24). The Enemy Way does the same work with its own songs and colors. The returning warrior has carried death on him, and he must be cleansed before he rejoins his people. James Adair saw something similar among the Chickasaw and Cherokee in the 1700s and listed it among his Israelite parallels. Three peoples, one instinct: war leaves a stain that the community must wash away.

**The house of the dead.** The Diné kept away from the dead. Traditionally a hogan where someone died was abandoned, and a hole might be broken in its north wall to carry the body out. The dead could leave behind a dangerous presence, and the living did not linger. The Torah has its own strict rule: when a person dies in a tent, everyone who enters and everything open inside is unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:14–16). Neither people treats death as ordinary. Both draw a hard line around it.

**The door that faces the dawn.** Every traditional hogan opens to the east, so the first thing that enters in the morning is the light. The Tabernacle also opened to the east. Moses, Aaron and the priests camped on that side, in front of the entrance (Exodus 27:13–16; Numbers 3:38). In Ezekiel's vision the glory of God comes back to the Temple through the east gate (Ezekiel 43:1–4). For the Diné and for Israel, the holy direction is where the sun rises.

**A land with borders given from above.** Israel's land came with edges drawn by God: from this river to that sea, from this mountain to that wadi (Numbers 34:1–12). The Diné homeland came with edges too, the four sacred mountains, set by the Holy People. In both cases the land is not just property. It is the place where the covenant, or the right way of life, can be kept.

**Exile and homecoming.** In 1864 the U.S. Army burned Diné fields and drove some 10,000 Diné and Mescalero Apache on the Long Walk to Bosque Redondo, a camp the Diné call Hwéeldi. Up to 3,500 died during the march and the years of confinement. In 1868 the American commissioners offered the survivors land in the Indian Territory to the east. The leader Barboncito answered that the Diné creator had told them never to go east of the Rio Grande. They chose a smaller piece of their own land over a richer exile, and that June they walked home.

This is the shape of Israel's story too. A shepherd people, driven from their land, held in a place they did not choose, then sent home. The returning exiles in the Psalm say they were like people dreaming, their mouths full of laughter (Psalm 126:1–2). Israel was commanded to recite its suffering and its rescue every year when it brought its first fruits (Deuteronomy 26:5–9). The Diné tell the Long Walk to their children in the same way, as the story that made them who they are.

There is even a detail about sheep. In Egypt, Joseph warned his brothers that shepherds were hated there (Genesis 46:34). At Bosque Redondo the Diné were told to become farmers on bad land. The treaty that sent them home promised them sheep and goats. The flocks came back with the people.

**Named by clan and house.** When Moses counted Israel, each man was listed "by their clans, by their fathers' houses" (Numbers 1:2, 18). An Israelite knew himself by tribe, clan and father's house. A Diné knows herself by four clans. The Kyrgyz, another people on our map, are expected to name seven generations of their fathers. The rules differ. Israel counts through the father, the Diné through the mother. But all three peoples treat kinship as a map you must be able to recite, and all three forbid marrying too close.

**The flood and the giants.** A people rescued from a rising flood. A pair of brothers who go up to their father and return to kill giants who terrorize the land. The Hebrew Bible has the flood (Genesis 6–8), the spies who saw giants in the land and felt like grasshoppers (Numbers 13:33), Joshua clearing the Anakim from the hill country (Joshua 11:21–22) and David facing Goliath (1 Samuel 17). The Diné story is its own story, with its own logic. But it carries the same deep memory: the world was once flooded and once full of giants, and the people survived both.

**Beauty and peace.** The Blessingway ends with words that mean, roughly, "in beauty it is finished." Hózhó is not just pretty. It is wholeness, everything in its right place. The Hebrew word shalom covers the same ground: peace, health, completeness. The priests of Israel blessed the people with it: may God turn his face toward you and give you peace (Numbers 6:24–26).

## Where a skeptic will push back

A fair reader will push back hard, and the objections are serious.

First, language and genetics point north and west, not east. Diné is an Athabaskan language, and its relatives are spoken in the Subarctic. Archaeologists and linguists date the ancestors' arrival in the Southwest to roughly 500 to 600 years ago. A 2008 Y-chromosome study led by Ripan Malhi found that a small group of Subarctic migrants founded the Navajo and Apache, and that many men from neighboring Southwestern peoples joined them later. Their lineages are Native American, with roots in Asia that go back to the first peopling of the Americas. No genetic study has found an Israelite signal among the Diné, and the Institute does not claim one.

Second, much of Diné ceremonial life was shaped by their Pueblo neighbors after they arrived: farming, weaving, sandpainting and parts of the ritual year. A custom that came from the Pueblos is a poor witness to an ancient journey from the Near East.

Third, fear of the dead, purification of warriors and east-facing doors are found among many peoples who never touched Israel. Anthropologists would call these common human responses to death, war and the sun, not signs of a shared origin.

Fourth, the Israelite claim among the Diné came from Latter-day Saint teaching, not from Diné tradition. Most Diné tell their origin through the emergence story, and many resent outsiders rewriting it. George P. Lee's painful break is a warning about how heavy an imported identity can become.

The Institute takes all of this seriously. Our claim is smaller and, we think, sturdier: that the Hebrew Bible and Diné tradition share a way of seeing the world, in which land, kinship, purity and memory are sacred, and that seeing them side by side helps each one make sense.

## A close

The Diné walked home in 1868 and have never left. Their mountains still stand at the four edges of the world. Their singers still cleanse the warriors. Their children still learn their clans before they learn much else.

Whatever their distant origins, they have kept something the prophets of Israel cared about: a people who remember where they belong, who walked back to it, and who still pray to walk in beauty.
