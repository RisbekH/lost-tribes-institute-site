A painter does not make a masterpiece with one color. God didn’t either. From the beginning, He designed humanity as a family of nations, and He never once asked those nations to stop being themselves.

Look at how the story starts. After Babel, God divided the peoples into nations, each with its own land and language (Deuteronomy 32:8; Genesis 10). Paul later explained why. God made every nation from one man and set their times and boundaries "so that they would seek Him and perhaps reach out and find Him, though He is not far from any of us" (Acts 17:26–27). The nations were not an accident to be corrected. They were a plan. Each one was placed where it was so that it could find its way back to its Maker.

That is why God also placed "eternity in their hearts" (Ecclesiastes 3:11). Don Richardson spent his life documenting this. People after people carried memories of the one Creator, and customs and stories that pointed ahead to a coming deliverer. The Sawi had their peace child. The Kyrgyz have their feasts, their epics, and their stories of Kydyr, the wandering one who matches scripture’s Jesus. Japan has its shrines paralleling biblical structures and capturing messianic symbolism. God did not leave the nations empty. He planted keys in every culture and waited for the one who would open them.

When that one came, he met with the nations: a Samaritan woman at a well (John 4), a Roman officer whose faith, he said, was greater than any he had found in Israel (Matthew 8).” He healed the daughter of a Phoenician woman (Mark 7). Long before Him, the prophet Elisha sent the Syrian captain Naaman home to Syria after he believed (2 Kings 5). Not one of them was told to become Jewish. They believed inside their own world, and their faith pleased God.

His first followers learned the same lesson, sometimes the hard way. When non-Jews began trusting the Messiah, some leaders wanted them circumcised, which in that day meant converting to Judaism. The council in Jerusalem refused to put that weight on them (Acts 15). Paul made it a rule for every community he served: if you were circumcised when God called you, stay that way; if you were not, don't become so. "Each one should remain in the situation they were in when God called them" (1 Corinthians 7:17–20). Paul had Timothy, partly Jewish, circumcised so he could live fully as a Jew, but he refused to let anyone circumcise Titus, a Greek. Titus was not Timothy, and Paul would not make him Jewish. Paul wasn't building a religion. He was gathering God’s diverse children into a family under one head.

Then came a beautiful sign at Pentecost. People from every nation heard the message "each in their own language" (Acts 2:6). God did not make them learn Hebrew first. He didn’t speak to them in sanctified language. He came to them in their mother tongue. That is the brilliance of God. He does not erase a culture to redeem it. He translates His Word into every language and incarnates Himself among all nations through His emissaries. Jesus, the Protagonist, fits every culture as if designed for that specific people.

Religious conversion, by contrast, has a sad record. When missionaries of any religion, whether Christian, Muslim, Jewish, Buddhist or Hindu, pull people out of their culture, they usually bring foreign rules, foreign words and foreign clothes. They call ancient customs evil and leave a people cut off from their own grandparents. They also hand the convert their religion's history of bloodshed and pride. Switching religions makes no one clean. Only one thing does: the life the Messiah gave in our place, offered to the whole world, "so that whoever believes in him will not perish" (John 3:16).

So what changes when someone comes home to God? The heart changes, and so does the voice a person follows. The people of Ephesus burned their sorcery books (Acts 19), and no one forced them. What does not change is their nationality, their name, their language, or their love for their own people.

The end of the story proves the point. John saw a great crowd before God's throne "from every nation, tribe, people and language" (Revelation 7:9). They still had their nations. They still spoke their languages. Heaven is not one culture. It is all of them, gathered and redeemed, each color still shining.

That is a God worth loving. He is wise enough to design a world of many peoples, patient enough to plant His keys in every one, and generous enough to meet each nation where it stands. He does not ask the Kyrgyz to become Americans, the Japanese to become Jews, or Hindus to become Westerners. He asks each of us to turn our hearts to Him, right where we are, and to live out Jesus’ international teachings as light among our own people.
