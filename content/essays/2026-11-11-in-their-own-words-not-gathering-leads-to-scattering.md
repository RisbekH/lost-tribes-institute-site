The prophets had promised a shepherd who would search for God’s scattered flock, gather the lost, and bring them home (Ezekiel 34).

Yeshua said: “He who is not with Me is against Me, and he who does not gather with Me scatters” (Matthew 12:30). Jerusalem’s high priest added that one would die not only for the nation, “but also for the scattered children of God, to gather them together into one” (John 11:52). Gathering was the Anointed One’s destiny.

He also expressed the conflict and pain of his calling. “O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, who kills the prophets and stones those sent to her, how often I have longed to gather your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, but you were unwilling! Look, your house is left to you desolate” (Matthew 23:37–38). He wept over the city and told her why the stones would topple: “because you did not recognize the time of your visitation from God” (Luke 19:44).

Despite the consensus of scholars, there is substantial reason to believe the complete collection of these and other statements about the temple’s destruction had been penned before Rome attacked Jerusalem in 70 AD.

This essay focuses on what happens to people who refuse the one sent to gather them. Lost Tribes Institute writes this paper because we see the same individual is still gathering people among all the nations. We also see the Jews as a template for other nations in the statement “to the Jews first and then the nations.” So our question is what happens to those who don’t gather with the one sent to gather.

I am not going to answer from the New Testament. The rabbis answer it themselves. They do not name Yeshua in this record. They do not have to. Their words are enough.

## The house

Their own sages taught that for forty years before the Second Temple was destroyed, the Temple’s signs of divine favor and acceptance failed. The lot “for God” on the Day of Atonement “did not arise in the High Priest’s right hand at all.” The strip of crimson wool that was to turn white when the people’s sins were forgiven “did not turn white.” The westernmost lamp, a sign that God’s presence rested on the nation, “did not burn continually.” The Sanctuary doors opened by themselves (Yoma 39a–39b).

Do the math. The Temple fell in the year 70. Forty years earlier is about the year 30. The Talmud never says what happened that year. Anyone who has read the Script knows.

Then the house fell. Why? Jewish Scripture had already recorded why the first temple fell 650 years earlier. It was decimated because the people had rejected and mocked those whom God had sent (2 Chronicles 36:15-16). The Jewish Talmud says it fell to baseless hatred, even though the people were busy with Torah, the commandments, and kindness (Yoma 9b). The same Talmud offers the explanation via the story of a Jew publicly humiliated by other Jews while the sages sat by and did nothing. Rome arrived in the wake of that Jew on Jew contempt (Gittin 55b–56a). The story is about a man named Bar Kamtza, a name that portrays its own message. Nonetheless, central to this story is a Jew shamed by his own people while the teachers kept silent, and then, as a consequence, the temple was destroyed. Readers notice the parallels to Yeshua.

Once the altar was gone, worship could not stay what it had been. Standing at the ruins, Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai told Rabbi Joshua, who was grieving the place where atonement had been made for Israel: “be not grieved, for we have another means of atonement which is as effective, and that is, the practice of lovingkindness” (Avot de-Rabbi Natan 4). Another teaching said that when Israel reads the order of the offerings, God will credit them “as though they had sacrificed them before Me and I will pardon them” (Ta’anit 27b).

The Torah required innocent blood on the altar while the prophets compared the best acts of God’s people to a used tampon (Isaiah 64:6). Now there was no temple, no altar, no high priest, no sacrifice, and no blood. The way a whole people approached God had to be rebuilt without its core, so they leaned into their good deeds which are like a menstrual rag.

On the other hand, those who had gathered with Yeshua were not waiting for fire to redefine the Script. They had come to the Script’s endpoint - the Messiah. They saw him as the initiation of the promised new covenant. As such, he was the temple, the high priest, the sacrifice, and the blood of the messianic era characterized by the fulfillment of Jewish scripture.

God had promised “a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah” (Jeremiah 31). The New Testament states, “By speaking of a new covenant, He has made the first one obsolete; and what is obsolete and aging will soon disappear” (Hebrews 8:13). The sacrifices were “only a shadow.” “It is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins” (Hebrews 10:1, 4). “Day after day every priest stands to minister and to offer again and again the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. But when this Priest (Yeshua) had offered for all time one sacrifice for sins, He sat down at the right hand of God” (Hebrews 10:11–12). “And where these have been forgiven, an offering for sin is no longer needed” (Hebrews 10:18). That is not a new religion invented from ashes. It is where the Tanakh points, written clearly before the temple fell - written by Jews who had joined the Protagonist’s gathering.

The others, however, gathered themselves some sixty years after the Temple burned. A group of rabbis including the well-known Rabbi Akiva, the teacher who did as much as anyone to shape the road ahead, gathered to redefine Judaism without the temple. Akiva looked for a messiah and pronounced Bar Kokhba as the one, stating a messianic promise, “A star will come forth from Jacob, and a scepter will arise from Israel” (Numbers 24:17). Akiva’s own student records this decision: “When Rebbi Aqiba (Akiva) saw Bar Koziba he said, this is King Messias.”

Not every sage agreed with Akiva, but the direction had been set. The community revolted against Rome with their messiah leading the way. The revolt failed, masses died, and survivors scattered. The Mishnah lists this defeat and the destruction of both the first and second temples among the disasters that took place on the Ninth of Av. Later, Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi would expound that Kokhba wasn’t a star (kokhav) but a fraud (kozav) (Eikhah Rabbah 2:4). Jewish literature remembers Akiva’s messiah as Bar Koziba. Son of a lie.

Putting it together: the signs stopped. The temple fell. The people scattered. The sages blamed their own baseless hatred, referencing Jews shaming a Jew. Atonement was remade without blood. The star turned out to be a lie. And the people scattered again. Every piece of that comes from their own books.

The two movements we met today had been one nation that split. One half grew from those who gathered with Yeshua and stayed on the path the Script had already set. The other grew from those who would not gather to Yeshua. They lost the Temple. They had to change how atonement was made. And they set the hope on their own goodness and a messiah their own sages later called a fraud. Is this now the template for non-Jewish nations? They either embrace scripture entirely, letting Heaven’s Shepherd bring them back to God or break from the Script’s trajectory with similar consequences?

Iran is scripture’s case study of this template. Iran basically adhered to the trajectory from Daniel and then Esther to the Magi and Pentecost resulting in the formation of the Yeshua movement’s Asian center. Sadly, when Iran’s Sassanian dynasty broke from the Shepherd, wolves scattered Persians like sheep without a shepherd. Today, Iran desperately seeks deliverance from those wolves.

He who does not gather, scatters.
