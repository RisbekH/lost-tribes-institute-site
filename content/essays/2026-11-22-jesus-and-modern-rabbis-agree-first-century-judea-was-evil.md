Jesus and the rabbis who came after him reach the same verdict on first-century Judea. In Luke 11 the Messiah looks at the crowds pressing around him and says, "This is an evil generation. It seeks after a sign. No sign will be given to it but the sign of Jonah, the prophet" (Luke 11:29). A few verses later he warns that "the blood of all the prophets" will be "required of this generation" (Luke 11:50). The rabbinic tradition never names Jesus, but it lands in the same place. The Talmud admits that the people of the Second Temple period studied Torah and kept the commandments. It still asks why the Temple was destroyed, and answers: "Because therein prevailed hatred without cause." Modern rabbis have not softened that judgment. Rav Abraham Isaac Kook, the first Chief Rabbi of the land of Israel, wrote: "If we were destroyed, and the world with us, due to baseless hatred…" A rabbi writing in Israel National News is just as blunt: "The Second Temple was destroyed on account of Sinat Chinam, baseless hatred." These two perspectives from "opposing sides" align on the topic of the character of Jews during Jesus' time. One is the Anointed One standing among that generation, and the other is the rabbinic tradition looking back at the ruins, condemning to whom Jesus addressed, "The Queen of the South will rise up with the men of this generation and condemn them, for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon. And look, one greater than Solomon is here" (Luke 11:31). Both Jesus and the Rabbis say that something in that generation had gone badly wrong, and that the ruin of Jerusalem in 70 AD originated from inside Israel, not from Rome who implemented heaven's decree.
