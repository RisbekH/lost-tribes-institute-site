## Introduction

In the West, stories persisted of the Ten Tribes of Israel (often misnamed Jews) living on the other side of the Sambatyon River, a mythical river that has no water but instead flows with sand and rocks. Some stories had a strong prince of these Ten Tribes, all living among a people with "no noses." The river could be the Taklamakan Desert, whose name, Jewish researcher Dr. Avigdor Shachan says, can be read in Hebrew as *Tachlim-Kan*, "You will be destroyed here."[^1] The people with "no noses"[^2] bring to mind the flatter faces of the Han Chinese.

In 1860, Dr. McGowan read a paper before the British Association stating that he had found evidence "of a numerous colony of Jews, in the city of Chengdu" about a century before Christ.[^3] Torrance adds that it had long been supposed that "Jews"[^4] had made their way to China several centuries before Christ.

Contemporary researchers have found evidence of large populations of Israelites in China. Esther Chen, in her paper *The Hidden Qiang: "Sons of Sheep,"* and Prof. Howard Ha-Sung Chung, in his paper "Israelites in China" and his book *Dispersion of the Israelites in the Eurasian Continent*, both say that long ago Israelites were part of the historical record of migrations and settlements in early China, East Asia and the Silk Road. While the larger populations have lost any memory of their Hebrew past, a smaller group gathered in the hills west of Chengdu still carried some memories. According to Torrance, this community still existed in 1937 and kept the traditions, chants and customs of their Israelite ancestors. He wrote many articles and a book about these people, whom he called the Ch'iang Min or Q'iang Min, living west of Chengdu in the high hills along the Min River valley.

Other presenters at this first Scientific Symposium will focus on their own areas of expertise: the Hata tribal migrations into Japan and the many biblical traditions inside the Shinto religion; the Manchu dynasty of China and its possible links to the tribe of Manasseh through the Yuezhi royal family;[^5] and the Manas Epic of Kyrgyzstan, in which Richard Hewitt has found over three hundred parallels to biblical stories.

In this paper I focus on the hill-tribe peoples of southwestern China, northern Myanmar and northeast India: their Israelite-like customs, chants and traditions; their connecting "Lost Book" legends; and, for the Lushai tribes, the cave-dwelling legend that connects them to Xinlong in China, west of Chengdu. I also explore the Lushai ancestral name Manasi, a parallel story of a "big wall" in the Kyrgyz Manas Epic, and some linguistic similarities.

### The Jews are not the Ten Tribes of Israel

To understand the core of this Scientific Symposium, we must see that "Jews," "Judaism" and "modern Israel" are distinct and separate from the dispersal of the Ten Tribes of Israel 2,763 years ago (around 740–721 BC). To conflate the two is to miss an important key to understanding biblical history and prophecy.[^6] In the map above, Rabbi Eliyahu Avichail[^7] tracks the Ten Tribes of Israel along the lower eastward route. He doesn't expect them to be Jews or to follow Judaism, but he says they will have Israelite customs and traditions. The Ten Tribes of Israel, also known as the House of Israel or the northern kingdom, went into captivity while they were acknowledging other gods and worshipping in groves on hilltops. This Scientific Symposium will not focus on biblical prophecy (except in passing) but on evidence for the historical dispersion and later migrations of the Ten Tribes of Israel, and on some of the places they live today. ### The Karen of northeastern Myanmar (formerly Burma)

In the early 1800s, deep in the jungles of northern Burma, the missionaries Dr. and Mrs. Mason found remarkable ideas of redemption, and stories of creation and the flood, among the hill-tribe people of the Sgaw Karen. They concluded that the Karen were part of the ten "lost" tribes of Israel.[^8] Others studying Karen culture found that crossing a river of sand was an important event in their oral history, and that they came from a land they called Thibi Kawbi, which may mean Tibet and the Gobi Desert. Of particular interest is how the Karen date their history:

> "The Karen reckon 2010 to be their year 2749. This means that they look to BC 739 as the year of their founding."[^9]

It was at precisely this time that the two and a half tribes of Israel living east of the Jordan River, Reuben, Gad and the half-tribe of Manasseh, went into captivity.[^10] Can this be a coincidence, given the other evidence?

The Karen had many unusual traditions about the origin of the human race, the fall, the flood and more. An early missionary account says:

> "It is supposed by some that the Karens are part of the lost tribes of Israel. How numerous would be the members of these tribes were the sum of all who are set down as belonging to them told! It certainly is convenient when you meet with a people whom you cannot account for, to give 'the lost tribes' the benefit of the doubt. There is, however, this to be said for the above-mentioned theory, that Dr. and Mrs. Mason found very remarkable and accurate traditions regarding the creation, the fall, and future regeneration of man."[^11]

As mentioned above, Dr. McGowan spoke of a colony of Israelites west of Chengdu 2,100 years ago, some of whom in all probability made their way to the mountains between China and Burma. He believed that they were either the ancestors of the Karen or the people through whom the Karen received their Hebrew Scripture traditions.[^12]

The discovery of the Karen and their Hebrew Scripture traditions must have caused quite a stir in Britain in the mid-1800s, because I have found references to them in other research books of the time. George Moore, M.D., writes of these Karen traditions in his 1861 book. Here is one of many examples (see also Appendix II):

> The coming of the Karen king, bringing the holy mountain, is associated with the expectation of blessedness to all nations, as in Isaiah.
>
> "When the Karen king arrives<br>
> There will be but one monarch, —<br>
> There will be neither rich nor poor.<br>
> Everything will be happy;<br>
> The beasts will be happy,<br>
> Lions and leopards will lose their savageness."
>
> That the Karens did not derive their ideas from a Christian source is evident from the fact that they are not trusting to a Saviour that has come, but that is coming.[^13]

### The Q'iang or Ch'iang Min of southwestern China

In the early 1900s a Scottish missionary named Torrance found a people with strongly Israelite customs on the ridges of the steep mountains around the Min River, near Wenchuan in western Sichuan. He wrote about them at length in his book *China's First Missionaries: Ancient Israelites*. His son wrote in the foreword to the 1988 second edition:

> "My father was deeply impressed with the Chiang way of life and with their monotheistic religion which stood out in marked contrast to the prevailing idolatry of the Chinese. But it was the biblical nature of their faith and worship, and the detailed correspondence between their religious observances and the teaching of the Old Testament, that captivated him."[^14]

The many similarities include an ancestor called "Bae-lan." A Ch'iang scholar says this is a transliteration in which "Bae" approximates the sound "Ab" and "lan" stands for "ram," which he takes as proof that Abram was their ancestor. There is also a tradition that their ancestor had twelve sons. Then there are the sacrifices, the architecture, the groves and the high places, all strongly similar to Semitic practices in ancient Palestine. Without listing all the parallels, Torrance concludes:

> "But finally when we found a tradition among them of a brazen serpent in the wilderness, and further secured a very old priestly Rod bearing upon it the image of a serpent, we felt that this, together with so much accumulated evidence, made the deduction legitimate that their religion was of Semitic origin."

Torrance says the Ch'iang Min "arrived several hundred years before the time of Christ."[^15] His son, Thomas F. Torrance, states that "their own chanted sagas and iliads handed down from father to son in their priestly families told of long wanderings through the steppes of Central Asia."[^16]

### Kyrgyzstan and the Epic of Manas

Richard Hewitt lived in rural Kyrgyzstan for twenty years, far from the Judeo-Christian world. There he found many striking biblical traditions. A comparison of Kyrgyzstan's national epic, *Manas*, with the Hebrew Scriptures strengthened the bond. Especially obvious is the similarity between the biblical patriarch "Manasseh son of Jacob" and the Kyrgyz hero "Manas son of Jakyb." Manas also leads an exodus comparable to Moses' exodus; strikingly, both leaders moved their people with 600,000 foot soldiers. His 2012 book, *Manas – Lost & Found*, covers an unending stream of similarities. Richard Hewitt is a keynote speaker at this first Scientific Symposium, and his papers are available alongside this one. He writes:

> "Myths portray lost Israeli tribes in Central Asia. DNA, linguistics, musical, and literary matches also reinforce evidence that 'Manasseh son of Jacob', who left the Near East's slopes 2700 years ago is the same 'Manas son of Jakyb' we see in Kyrgyzstan's national epic."[^17]

## Lost Book stories: northeast India to Central Asia

Anthropologist Don Richardson documented ten hill-tribe peoples in and around northern Myanmar who have stories of a Lost Book.[^24] Torrance tells of the Ch'iang Min performing a ritual like the Day of Atonement, and of a priest exclaiming that the Bible was their lost scriptures.[^25] The Altai of southern Siberia have a lost golden book,[^26] and the Kyrgyz say the Kitai Khan, the emperor of northern China, burned their book.[^27]

### The Karen Lost Book

The Karen version of the Lost Book, according to Reverend Baw Ney,[^28] goes like this:

> After Y'wa created the earth he decided to go on a long journey, and so he called his sons together and gave each a book of life. To the Karen, the eldest, he gave a Golden Book of Life. To the others he gave other books of life, until finally to the white man, the youngest brother, he gave a White Book of Life. The white brother took his White Book of Life and went away to the west, and was never seen again. As long as the Karen read and followed his Golden Book of Life, his life was happy and prosperous.
>
> One day the Karen brother was burning and clearing a field in the forest. He put his Golden Book of Life on a stump in the field while he was doing his work. In his carelessness, the Golden Book of Life was burned, leaving only fragments that he brought back to his house. Gradually the older brother neglected these fragments, until one day they fell through the cracks of the bamboo floor in his house, and the pigs and chickens underneath ate them up. When he no longer had his book of life, his life became more and more wretched with fears, sickness, crop failures, and persecutions from outsiders.[^29]

The Karen brother hoped that someday his white younger brother would come back on the wings of a great white bird floating on the water and bring his White Book of Life to share with his older brother. The Karen waited for him to restore the prosperity that Y'wa had intended for them from the beginning.[^30]

### Nine other hill tribes

The Karen are not alone in their Lost Book tradition. Nine other hill tribes in the region have similar oral traditions.[^31]

- **Kachin:** "The Kachin, like the Karen, believe that Karai Kasang once gave their forefathers a book which they lost." Karai Kasang is their name for a kind supernatural Being "whose shape and form exceeds man's ability to comprehend."[^32]
- **Lahu:** Their tradition says Gui'Sha, the Creator of all things, gave their ancestors a law written on rice cakes, and during a famine they were forced to eat the rice cakes to survive.[^33] The Lahu also built "long houses" for the awaited return of the book, which can still be found in their villages today.
- **Wa:** The Wa await a white brother with their Lost Book.[^34]
- **Shan and Palaung:** These peoples look to a fifth manifestation of the Buddha, Phra Ariya-Metrai, who was spoken of in their scriptures, which were lost in war in Laos.[^35]
- **Kui:** Kui tribesmen expected a messenger who would bring their Lost Book back to special places of worship built for that event.[^36]
- **Lisu:** The Lisu of Yunnan province, China, believe a king of their own will one day rule over them and restore their book in their own language, even though the Lisu had no writing or alphabet.[^37]
- **Naga:** The Naga consist of twenty-four tribes, and at least one, the Rengma, remembers that "the Supreme Being gave His words to their forefathers by writing them on animal skins. But the forefathers did not take care of the skins. Dogs ate them up!"[^38]
- **Mizo:** Mizo researcher Zaithanchhungi writes: "According to the oral tradition of Mizo, the leather scrolls in which all ancestral history of the tribes and families were written, were lost due to continuous slavery. The leather Scrolls in which they had written about their origins, family histories and narratives of their travels were said to have been left behind in their labour camps when they fled China. According to another legend of the Mizo, the leather Scrolls were eaten by a dog of the keeper of the Scrolls."

Dr. Khuplam Milui Lenthang records this Kuki-Chin-Mizo *themthu* (song) about crossing the Jordan River:

> I crossed the river with a narrow passed<br>
> Be united and strong like a refined iron and<br>
> Carry it like a trophy with great joy and pride<br>
> We could not be popular since we have so many enemies<br>
> I fought many battles and wars continuously<br>
> We are the children of Manmasu<br>
> Aboriyah.[^39]

Lenthang also writes that two missionary couples during British rule were amazed by the similarities between Kuki-Chin-Mizo culture and customs and those of biblical Israel. Although the people had lost their scroll, they had developed games that helped keep these customs and culture alive.[^40]

### The burning of the books: China, 213 BC

Zaithanchhungi offers this explanation. In 213 BC, the Emperor Shih Huang Ti burned all books except those on agriculture and medicine in his drive to unify China. He also relocated people en masse[^41] to remove every philosophy other than his own, known as Legalism. He may have been given the insulting title of "dog," and so over time the emperor who burned their books may have become the "dog" who destroyed their books, and eventually the dog who ate their scroll.[^42]

Britannica says of Shih Huang Ti:

> "As emperor he initiated a series of reforms aimed at establishing a fully centralized administration, thus avoiding the rise of independent satrapies. Construction of a network of roads and canals was begun, and fortresses erected for defence against barbarian invasions from the north were linked to form the Great Wall."[^43]

Britannica continues:

> "Confucian scholars strongly condemned Shihuangdi and it is said that 460 of them were executed for their opposition. The continuous controversy between the emperor and Confucian scholars who advocated a return to the old feudal order culminated in the famous burning of the books of 213, when, at Li Si's suggestion, all books not dealing with agriculture, medicine, or prognostication were burned, except historical records of Qin and books in the imperial library."[^44]

We need to ask whether this book burning etched itself into the collective memory of the Peoples of the Lost Book and was passed down through oral tradition.

### The Altai and their Golden Book

North of the Kyrgyz, the Altai have a legend of a Golden Book, just like the Karen. The Altai Golden Book is kept in a golden chest, reminiscent of the golden Ark of the Bible. In their version, the golden chest falls into the water while the people are crossing a river. They take out the golden book to dry its pages, a cow eats them, and the book is lost.[^47]

### The Q'iang Min and their lost scriptures

In 1925, Rev. Thomas T. Torrance found a Q'iang priest performing a ritual that resembled Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement of the children of Israel in the Torah. His son, Thomas F. Torrance, wrote in the foreword to the 1988 second edition of his father's book:[^48]

> "In 1925 I had the privilege of being present at the village of Oir, some ten thousand feet above sea level, situated in a lateral valley behind Weichou, when a venerable Chiang priest revealed to my father for the first time the full rite of the Day of Atonement in the Chiang liturgical year. When my father read to him the sixteenth chapter of the book of Leviticus, the priest leapt off his seat in excitement, exclaiming that these were the lost Chiang Scriptures."[^49]

This priest went on to identify himself and his family with the people of the Book that Torrance was reading from. So are the Peoples of the Lost Book one and the same as the people of the Book we know as the Bible?

## Manas, Manasi, Manmasi and the tribe of Manasseh

The name most often associated with these Israelite peoples is a form of Manasseh, the son of Joseph and one of the twelve tribes of Israel (one of the two sons Jacob adopted). Manas, Manmasi and Menashe are names that connect many of these peoples from Central Asia to northeast India. We have Manas of the Kyrgyz, the Altai and Inner Mongolia; the Manasi or Menashe of the Mizo; and the Manmasi of the Kuki. The Manchu people of China's last dynasty also appear to be named after Manasseh.

### Manas of Kyrgyzstan

The *Epic of Manas* tells the story of Manas, his descendants and their battles against various enemies; some versions run to about 500,000 lines.[^54] Richard Hewitt lived in rural Kyrgyzstan for twenty years and found hundreds of biblical parallels in the lines of this chanted epic while sipping warm horse milk with shepherds in their yurts. Manas is always the hero. One day an old shepherd leaned forward, looked him in the eye and said, "Do you want to know the name of Manas' father?" When Richard said yes, the shepherd replied, "Jakyb." It was then that Richard realized that Manas son of Jakyb was the same as Manasseh son of Jacob, one of the mythical "Lost Ten Tribes."

The Kyrgyz epic says that some of Jakyb's relatives were scattered to Manchuria and others to places "beyond Tibet":

> "Journeying in their places<br>
> The field of Manchuria,<br>
> On the other side of Tibet,<br>
> Not the slightest is heard from them."[^55]

The Kuki sing about a time when the Kitai separated them from their brothers on the Samakai Mountains:

> "Oh my clansmen, that we have been torn<br>
> Upon the big Samakai Range<br>
> My sadness spreads from north to south.<br>
> Like the water that is flowing, I move on."[^56]

North of Assam, which is "the other side of Tibet" from the Kyrgyz, lies the Manas River, surrounded by Manas National Park.

### Zaithanchhungi on Manasi in Mizo and Lushai tradition

The name Manasia or Manase is often cited in chants used in sacrifices and other sacred matters:[^57]

1. At a family offering: "The children of Manase offer Thee animal's blood, O God of High."
2. For someone who falls sick at the *jhum* (hill field): "If tied, untie him; if manacled, unlock him; we are taking Manase home."
3. At the sacrifice for epilepsy: "Release him, we are above your power, we are children of Manase."
4. On choosing a paddy field with something taboo: "Go away, go away, we are bringing Manase here."
5. On felling the first tree when clearing for a house: "You are obstructing our grandfather Manasia."
6. In oral history: "Whenever you pray to God, the name of our forefather Manasia must be pronounced."
7. At every sacrifice for sin, the priest chants: "God of heaven and of the earth, we, sons of Manasia, who are on earth are offering the heart, the liver of the animal for forgiveness of our sins."

### The Manchu and the people of southern China

Howard Ha-Sung Chung, another keynote speaker at this symposium, has done extensive research on the waves of Israelites flowing into and out of China. The subject is too detailed to do justice to here, so I refer the reader to his book.[^59] He argues that Manchu was not a place name but a tribal name. The Manchu lived among the former Jachinites and Hebrews, so "Manchu" may be a phonetic rendering of an Israelite tribe; Manasseh comes to mind.[^60] He also notes that the Yuezhi and Alans of Central Asia were called "Wen-na-sha" in Chinese literature; allowing for the shift from M to W, this gives the name Manasseh.[^61]

### The half-tribe of Manasseh

Remember that this tribe was so large by the time it reached the Promised Land of Canaan that it split in two. Half stayed on the east side of the Jordan, where the land was good for raising animals. Moses allowed this as long as they helped their brothers fight in Canaan. They were good fighters. Years later, along with two other tribes, they were taken captive in 740 BC and relocated to the northeastern border of what would later become the Persian Empire. Steven M. Collins told me that they became Persia's border guards in this area and went their own way when the Persian Empire fell. The Greek historian Herodotus calls them the Massagetae.[^62] Alexander the Great fought them, and many fled into Central Asia afterward. Many Central Asian countries tell the story of their warrior queen Tomyris defeating King Cyrus. Saka is an earlier name of the Israelites, from around the 8th century BC,[^63] and many historians write about the Saka and their exploits in Central Asia.

## The big wall

The Mizo remember escaping through a hole they dug in a big wall.[^64] It appears that many peoples were enslaved in China to build the Great Wall and many others like it.

The Kyrgyz Manas Epic also mentions a big wall. The story goes: "Jakyb's brother, Bai, from the other side of the Himalayas, was trying to visit Jakyb. He got caught en route and was enslaved to build the wall. He was old and lay in the ditch with dead captives and workers. When the guards left, he fled."[^65]

## Language

In 2019 I took three Mizos to the World Epic Festival in Bishkek. While getting ready to be interviewed, the Mizos heard two Kyrgyz speak a very familiar phrase: "tok to, tok to," meaning "stop fussing." The sound and intonation were the same as the Mizo phrase with the same meaning, "tawk tawh, tawk tawh."[^66] Surely the Mizo and the Kyrgyz were once one and the same people.

Richard Hewitt records some Kuki–Kyrgyz word similarities from an interview with Tsvi Khaute in Jerusalem in 2005:[^67]

- *Kut*: an object of blessing (Kyrgyz); a day of celebration (Kuki)
- *Mizam*: law or commandment (Kyrgyz)
- *Bulpizam*: ancient writing (Kyrgyz); the Torah or original book (Kuki)

A comparative linguistic study of the Kyrgyz and Kuki/Mizo languages is needed.

## Conclusion

These examples of peoples with a "lost book" legend, from Kyrgyzstan through China to northeast India, show an overwhelming need for further research. Not only do we have these peoples remembering their Lost Book, and the peoples at either end of the route, in Kyrgyzstan and northeast India, carrying a similar name for their ancestor (Manas, Manasi, Manmasi), but we also have remarkable timing: the Karen date themselves from the same year that the half-tribe of Manasseh and two other tribes of Israel were taken captive and relocated somewhere east of Persia. We also have the Kuki memory, in poem form, of crossing the Red Sea; the Q'iang Min of Sichuan, China, performing Yom Kippur rituals; and the many references to the biblical patriarchs in *The Epic of Manas*, as Richard Hewitt has documented here and in his books. Surely the legend of the Peoples of the Lost Book must have some connection to the people of "The Book." This Scientific Symposium may well be the key to unlocking more of these truths, and so to building a fuller picture of where and who the Lost Tribes of Israel are in the eastern hemisphere.
