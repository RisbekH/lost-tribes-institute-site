A man runs along the black lava of the Kona coast. He ate with the women, or let his shadow fall on a chief, or touched what was kapu, forbidden. The penalty is death, and the chief's men are behind him. Ahead lies a great wall of fitted stone, ten feet high, and beyond it a temple with carved wooden images staring out to sea. If he can swim the bay and get inside the wall, no one may touch him. A priest will see to him. In a few days he will walk home a free man.

That was the puʻuhonua at Hōnaunau, on the island of Hawaiʻi. It sheltered kapu-breakers, defeated warriors, children, the old, and anyone else who could not fight.[^nps] When the missionary William Ellis walked the coast in 1823, he called it a "City of Refuge." He was a Bible reader, and he knew where that phrase came from.[^ellis]

## Who the Polynesians are

Polynesians are the sailors of the great triangle between Hawaiʻi, Aotearoa (New Zealand) and Rapa Nui (Easter Island). It was the last large region of the earth that humans settled. Most of their ancestry comes from Austronesian-speaking voyagers who set out from the Taiwan region thousands of years ago, mixed with Papuan-speaking peoples in Melanesia, and reached the open Pacific with the Lapita culture about 3,000 years ago.[^genes] Hawaiʻi was reached much later, around 900 to 1100 CE, probably from the Marquesas.[^nps]

In 2020 a team led by Alexander Ioannidis reported in *Nature* that several Polynesian island groups carry traces of contact with Native Americans from the region of today's Colombia, from around 1200 CE.[^ioannidis] The Pacific was a highway, not a wall.

Today Hawaiians, Tongans and Samoans live across the ocean and the world. Latter-day Saint missions arrived in Hawaiʻi in 1850 and taught that Polynesians are of the house of Israel, descended from Lehi or from the shipbuilder Hagoth of the Book of Mormon. Tonga and Samoa now have among the highest Latter-day Saint membership rates anywhere. The Native Hawaiian scholar Hokulani Aikau has shown how Hawaiian members at Lāʻie took that "chosen people" teaching and made their town a puʻuhonua of their own.[^aikau]

## Their own story

The Hawaiians did not need anyone to give them a genealogy. They had one. The *Kumulipo*, a chant of 2,102 lines composed in the 1700s for the chief Kalaninuiamamao, begins in Pō, the deep night. Out of the dark come the coral polyp, the shellfish, the seaweeds, the fish, the birds, the creeping things, then the gods, then people, and at last the line of chiefs down to the child the chant was made for. Queen Liliʻuokalani translated it while under house arrest in ʻIolani Palace and published it in 1897.[^kumulipo]

Another story explains why Hawaiians are bound to the land. Wākea, the sky father, had a child with Hoʻohōkūkalani. The baby was born dead. They buried him beside the house, and from the grave grew the first taro plant, the kalo. Their second son was named Hāloa after him, and he became the ancestor of the Hawaiian people. The elder brother feeds the younger, and the younger cares for the elder. That is why kalo is family.

Voyaging chants speak of Kahiki, the homeland far across the sea, and of navigators who sailed back and forth to it. The Hawaiʻi judge Abraham Fornander gathered many of these traditions in *An Account of the Polynesian Race* (1878–85) and set some of them, a first man and a great flood survived by Nuʻu, beside the stories of Genesis.[^fornander]

## The Institute's reading

What follows is the Institute's reading, not proven history. We set Hawaiian customs beside the Hebrew Bible and look at what mirrors.

**Places of refuge.** The Torah tells Israel to set apart six cities of refuge, three on each side of the Jordan, so that a person who has killed someone without intent can flee there and be safe from the avenger of blood (Numbers 35:9–15). The elders at the gate hear his case and take him in (Joshua 20:1–6). He goes home free only when the high priest dies (Numbers 35:25–28).

Hōnaunau reverses the order but keeps the bones of the idea. A holy enclosure, guarded by a temple that held the bones of chiefs, stops the hand of vengeance. A priest, not a judge, declares the fugitive clean. Then he goes home. The Cherokee had the same thing. The trader James Adair, writing in 1775, described their peace town of Chota as a "town of refuge" and set it beside the Hebrew cities. Three peoples, three oceans apart, built walls to stop the chain of blood.

**Kapu and holiness.** Hawaiian kapu is the Hebrew idea of the holy and the forbidden in another dress. Certain foods were forbidden to women. Men and women ate apart. Women withdrew to a separate house during their monthly blood, as Leviticus 15:19–24 sets a woman apart for seven days. Chiefs carried such sacred power that commoners lay face down as they passed, much as the Israelites kept their distance from Mount Sinai and from the ark (Exodus 19:12–13; 2 Samuel 6:6–7). The Māori, cousins of the Hawaiians and also on our map, call the same force tapu. In 1819, after the death of Kamehameha I, his widow Kaʻahumanu and his son Liholiho sat down and ate together in public, and the old kapu system fell.[^nps]

**A harvest season of peace.** Makahiki ran for four lunar months from about November, when the Pleiades rose at dusk. Chiefs' stewards gathered pigs, taro, sweet potatoes, dried fish, bark cloth and mats from each district and laid them before the god Lono. All war was banned while Lono's image was carried around the island.[^makahiki] Israel brought the first sheaf to God before anyone ate the new grain (Leviticus 23:10–14) and ended its year with the Feast of Booths, the great harvest gathering (Leviticus 23:39–43). In the seventh year the land itself rested (Leviticus 25:1–7). The Zulu, elsewhere on the map, held back the new crops until their king had tasted them first. Harvest, offering and a holy pause run through all three.

**The covenant cut.** Across Polynesia boys at the edge of manhood underwent a ritual cut. Samoans and Tongans call it *tefe*, from a Proto-Oceanic root meaning to circumcise; Hawaiians used *kahe*, "to slit."[^tefe] It was a slit of the foreskin, not its removal, and it came at puberty, not on the eighth day as in Genesis 17:12. Still, a cut in the same place marks a boy as belonging to his people, the sign God gave Abraham (Genesis 17:10–14) and that Joshua renewed at Gilgal before Israel entered its land (Joshua 5:2–9).

**Genealogy as identity.** The Kumulipo climbs from the first night to the living chief. Genesis climbs from Adam to Noah to Abraham (Genesis 5; 11:10–26), and Chronicles spends nine chapters on names before it tells a single story (1 Chronicles 1–9). The writers of Jesus' life open the same way, tracing his line back to David and Abraham. In both worlds you are who your fathers were, and the chant or the list is your title deed.

**Elder and younger.** Hāloa the taro is the elder brother. Hāloa the man is the younger, and he carries the line. The Hebrew Bible keeps telling this story: Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Ephraim over Manasseh (Genesis 48:13–20), Joseph over his older brothers. That last pair matters here, because the Latter-day Saint teaching makes the Polynesians sons of Joseph through Manasseh.

## Where a skeptic will push back

The strongest objection is time. Lapita voyagers reached the remote Pacific about 3,000 years ago, centuries before Assyria carried the northern tribes away around 722 BCE. Their ancestry runs back to the Taiwan region, not to the Levant. No published study has found Israelite or West Asian genetic markers in Polynesians. The one outside strand that geneticists have found points to South America.[^ioannidis]

Second, refuges, food taboos, harvest festivals, puberty cuts and long genealogies are found in many societies on every continent. Anthropologists read them as common human answers to blood feud, sacred power, food supply and coming of age. Likeness of custom alone does not prove shared descent.

Third, the Israel reading came to Hawaiʻi from outside. Ellis named the puʻuhonua with a Bible phrase. Fornander was an amateur working in the race theories of his day. The house-of-Israel teaching arrived with Latter-day Saint missionaries in 1850, and Aikau notes that it came bundled with ideas about race. Some Hawaiians ask why their ancestors should need a borrowed pedigree.

The Institute's answer is modest. We are not claiming the Lapita sailors were Israelites. We note that ideas, words and customs can travel by ship faster than genes, that the Pacific was crossed many times, and that the overlap is unusually dense. The question stays open.

## Close

The wall at Hōnaunau still stands. Visitors walk barefoot past the restored temple and the carved images and look out at the same bay the fugitives swam. Whatever their deep origin, the people who built it understood something the Torah also understood: that a community needs a place where vengeance stops and a person can be made clean and go home. That is worth honoring, in Hawaiian terms and in Hebrew ones.
