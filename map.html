World map · field register

Nations and peoples who say they come from ancient Israel

Communities, synagogues, churches, researchers, writers, university departments, and YouTube channels around the world argue that a particular people descends from the ancient tribes of Israel. This register collects those claims from every kind of source, from rabbinic recognitions and old travel accounts to self-published books and online video, and puts each one on the map.

Drag to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom. Tap a marker to jump to its entry.

Each entry records what the people making the claim say and why. Inclusion does not mean the claim is proven. Only a few of these groups (Beta Israel, Bnei Menashe, Bene Israel, the Samaritans) have recognition from Jewish religious authorities, and genetic studies have tested only a handful. Tribe names follow what each group or its promoters assert.

Method

Why readers of Scripture see these parallels

The Lost Tribes Institute treats Scripture as anthropology's foundational text. Long before the word anthropology existed, the Bible recorded one people's customs in close detail over a thousand years. It covers their food laws, purity rules, marriage and inheritance, festivals and calendar, priesthood, tribal structure, warfare and burial. Genesis 10 adds a table of nations that tries to place every known people in one family tree.

A person who knows that record intimately carries a detailed reference set. When they meet a people who circumcise on the eighth day, set women apart after childbirth, keep a first-fruits festival, name towns of refuge, marry a dead brother's widow, or carry a sacred chest into battle, they recognize a pattern. An observer without that knowledge would see unrelated local customs. The comparison is only possible for someone who knows both sides well.

The history on this map bears that out. Almost every claim here was first noticed by someone steeped in Scripture:

1683
William Penn among the Lenape
1775
James Adair among the Chickasaw, Cherokee, Choctaw, Creek and Catawba
1800s
Missionaries among the Karen, Māori and Assyrians
1937
Thomas Torrance among the Qiang
Today
Arimasa Kubo in Japan; Margot Crossing and Richard Hewitt of the Lost Tribes Institute, from Kyrgyzstan to Mizoram; also the researcher Howard Chung

The Institute's founder, Margot Crossing, has carried this method across Asia. She traces a chain of “lost book” peoples, from the Kyrgyz through the Q'iang of Sichuan to the Karen, Chin, Kuki and Mizo, who each remember losing a sacred book and several of whom name their ancestor Manas, Manasi or Manmasi. Of the hill peoples and the Q'iang she writes:

“All of them had ‘Israelitish’ customs and folklore.”

Margot Crossing, “Mizoram: Lost Tribe of Israel”

Her conclusion from the oral histories, presented at the Bishkek symposium in 2019:

“No doubt these people of ‘The Lost Book’ are also people of ‘The Book’.”

Margot Crossing, “Lost Book Peoples” (2019)

The same happened when indigenous readers received the Bible in their own language. Mizo, Igbo, Zulu, Fijian and Ga readers recognized their own grandparents' customs in its pages, often before any outsider suggested a link.

Scripture also tells its readers to expect this. It says the northern tribes were settled in Halah, on the Habor river in Gozan, and in the towns of the Medes (2 Kings 17:6). It warns that Israel would be scattered from one end of the earth to the other (Deuteronomy 28:64). It promises a regathering from the islands of the sea and the four corners of the earth (Isaiah 11:11–12). A reader who takes these words seriously goes looking for remnants far from the land of Israel.

The strongest comparisons are specific rather than general, come in clusters rather than one at a time, and were recorded before missionaries arrived. Critics argue that some likenesses come from ways of life many peoples share, such as herding, clan loyalty and sacrifice, or from ideas missionaries brought. Each entry below records who made the claim and when, so readers can weigh each case for themselves.

    Scripture

    The promised return

    The term “Lost Tribes” never appears in Scripture. What the Prophets do say, many times, is that the scattered northern tribes will one day come back. These chapters, written mostly between the 8th and 5th centuries BC, foretell that return. Each one also carries messianic imagery that Jesus (Yeshua, Isa) later applied to himself.

    Reading them requires one piece of history. After Solomon's son Rehoboam, Israel's twelve tribes split in two (c. 930 BC):

    IsraelThe ten northern tribes, also called Ephraim, Joseph or Samaria. Deported by Assyria c. 740–720 BC and “lost.”
    JudahThe southern kingdom, also called Jerusalem. Exiled to Babylon and returned; today's Jews descend from Judah.

    When the Prophets name both houses, they are promising that the two will be reunited.

    Isaiah 11–12

    Read ↗
    He will lift up a banner for the nations, and assemble the dispersed of Israel, and gather the scattered of Judah from the four corners of the earth.Isaiah 11:12

    A shoot from Jesse, King David's father, carries the Spirit of the Lord. The Lord will redeem the remnant of his people “a second time” (11:11). Ephraim and Judah finally make peace (11:13), and a highway is made so the remnant can return.

    Echo“And this is eternal life, that they may know you” (John 17:3); “other sheep that are not of this fold” (John 10:16).

    Hosea 1–3

    Read ↗
    In the place where it was said to them, “You are not my people,” it shall be said to them, “Children of the living God.”Hosea 1:10

    Hosea is the only one of these prophets who preached in the north. God is the husband and Israel the wife who left him, yet he promises to take her back: “I will betroth you to me forever” (2:19). Judah and Israel are gathered under one head (1:11).

    EchoJesus calls himself the groom: “Can the wedding guests fast while the groom is with them?” (Mark 2:19).

    Isaiah 49

    Read ↗
    It is too small a thing for you to be my servant to restore the tribes of Jacob… I will also make you a light for the Gentiles.Isaiah 49:6

    A Servant Song addressed to “you islands” and “distant nations.” The Servant's mission is to bring Jacob back and gather Israel, then to reach the ends of the earth. The scattered children return, carried by the nations (49:22).

    Echo“I have engraved you on the palms of my hands” (49:16) is read as a picture of the nails.

    Jeremiah 31

    Read ↗
    I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah… they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest.Jeremiah 31:31, 34

    Perhaps the most iconic return chapter, inside a whole section (Jeremiah 30–33) about Israel's return. Both houses are named. The new covenant is written on the heart, and everyone will know the Lord.

    Echo“This is my blood of the new covenant, shed for many” (Matthew 26:28).

    Ezekiel 34

    Read ↗
    I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured.Ezekiel 34:16

    God himself searches for his scattered sheep and gathers them from the countries. Then the line between God the shepherd and “my servant David” blurs: “I will set up over them one shepherd” (34:23).

    Echo“I am the good shepherd… one flock, one shepherd” (John 10:11–16); “go to the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (Matthew 10:6).

    Ezekiel 36

    Read ↗
    I will take you from the nations and gather you from all the countries… a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you.Ezekiel 36:24, 26

    Israel's cities will be rebuilt and filled again. The people who were scattered among the nations are cleansed and given a new heart and God's Spirit. That, not a religion or ritual, is what marks them.

    Echo“The Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you all things” (John 14:26).

    Ezekiel 37

    Read ↗
    I am about to take the stick of Joseph (that is in the hand of Ephraim)… and I will join with it the stick of Judah, and make them one stick.Ezekiel 37:19

    Two visions. First, a valley of dry bones, the scattered house of Israel, comes back to life when the “son of man” calls the breath from the four winds. Second, the sticks of Joseph and Judah become one under a single king, David, with an everlasting covenant of peace.

    Echo“The Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost” (Luke 19:10).

    Isaiah 54

    Read ↗
    For a brief moment I deserted you, but with great compassion I will gather you.Isaiah 54:7

    The barren woman is told to enlarge her tent for a multitude of children. God is the husband who left for a moment and returns to renew his covenant of peace. Verse 13 adds, “All your children shall be taught by the Lord.”

    EchoThe groom and bride of Hosea return; the Messiah as bridegroom.

    More return passages: Isaiah 56, 60 and 63, Zechariah 9–11, and dozens of shorter verses.
